The 2% Rule: Hay Math Starts Here

Horse nutrition has one beautifully simple anchor: a horse at maintenance eats roughly 2% of its body weight in forage per day. A 1,000 lb horse needs about 20 lb of hay daily. A 500 kg horse needs about 10 kg. This is not a folk rule — it traces to the National Research Council's Nutrient Requirements of Horses, which frames intake as a percentage of body weight because a horse's gut is built to process a steady volume of fiber, not a fixed weight. Bigger horses need more material moving through the hindgut to keep fermentation — and everything downstream of it — working.

The 2% figure is a maintenance baseline for an idle adult horse. Easy keepers (the air-ferns of the horse world: ponies, Morgans, many Quarter Horses) hold weight at 1.5%. Hard keepers, late-pregnant mares, lactating mares, and horses in real work climb toward 2.5%. And these percentages describe total forage — if the horse gets grain, the hay portion drops accordingly. HayWise's intake selector covers the 1.5–2.5% range so you can match the horse in front of you, not the average horse in a textbook.

Dry Matter: The Detail That Changes the Number

Here is the subtlety most hay calculators skip: the 1.5–2.5% guideline is stated on a dry-matter basis, but you buy and weigh hay as fed — water included. Baled hay is typically 85–92% dry matter. So 20 lb of “hay” at 88% dry matter delivers only 17.6 lb of actual dry feed. Weigh out the dry-matter number directly and you short the horse by roughly a tenth of its ration, every day, all winter.

For planning purposes, most owners can fold this into a small buffer rather than doing the division explicitly — HayWise's 10% safety recommendation on bale counts covers it along with ordinary waste. But if you are weighing daily rations on a scale (and for easy keepers and metabolic horses, you should be), divide the dry-matter target by your hay's dry-matter fraction to get the as-fed weight you actually put in the net.

From Daily Ration to Winter: The Season Math

Winter hay planning is one of the few genuinely arithmetic problems in horse keeping: daily ration × days × horses, plus waste, divided by bale weight. Take one 1,000 lb horse at 2%: 20 lb/day. Over a 121-day winter (December through March): 2,420 lb. Add 5% storage waste and 5% feeding waste and you are at about 2,670 lb — which at 40 lb per small square bale is 67 bales.

The two numbers owners get wrong are days and bale weight. Days is not “winter” in the abstract — it is the stretch with no meaningful pasture, which in Minnesota runs October to May and in Georgia barely exists. Count your own dates. Bale weight is worse: “small squares” range from 35 to 60 lb depending on baler, crop, and how tight the farmer packs. Weigh three bales and average them; a 10 lb per-bale error compounds to half a ton across a winter.

Waste: The Invisible Second Horse

How you feed changes how much you must buy, dramatically. University of Minnesota research puts numbers on it: feeding small squares in a hay rack or feeder wastes about 5%; feeding on the ground wastes around 13%; large round bales fed without a feeder waste up to 57% — more than half the bale trampled, soiled, and refused. With a good round-bale feeder, waste drops to 5–33% depending on design.

Read that again: the feeder decision can be worth more hay than the horse's appetite. A $200 feeder that cuts waste from 30% to 10% pays for itself in a single winter for most herds. HayWise's waste presets (rack +5%, ground +13%, round-no-feeder +20% as a conservative middle) let you see the cost of your feeding method in bales, which is a powerfully motivating unit.

Cold Weather: Hay Is the Furnace

Horses do not heat themselves with grain; they heat themselves with hay. Fiber fermenting in the hindgut throws off substantial heat — the horse is a walking compost pile, in the best way. That is why the correct response to a cold snap is more forage, not more grain: below about 18°F, a horse in full winter coat needs roughly 2 extra pounds of hay per day for every 10 degrees colder. At -2°F, that is 4 extra pounds — a 20% bump on a 20 lb ration.

Wind and wet multiply the effect: a clipped horse, a horse without shelter, or a wet horse loses heat far faster and needs more. Blanketing reduces the requirement but does not eliminate it. The practical move is a cold-snap buffer: plan the season math at maintenance, then keep an extra week or two of hay on hand for the deep freezes. January hay costs more than July hay, and “we're out” in a blizzard is not a situation you want.

Bale Types: Squares, Rounds, and the Trade-Offs

Small squares (35–60 lb) are the most expensive per ton but the most controllable: easy to weigh, easy to portion, minimal waste with a rack, and no equipment needed. They are the right choice for one or two horses, for easy keepers on measured rations, and for anyone without a tractor.

Large squares (700–1,000+ lb) and round bales (800–1,200 lb) are far cheaper per ton but demand equipment to move and a feeder to avoid catastrophic waste. Rounds have a second weakness: their shape sheds rain from the outside but wicks it into the bottom, so the outer 4–6 inches often spoil — factor that in, and never feed the moldy rind. One more round-bale caution: horses eating with their heads buried in a round bale for weeks can develop respiratory irritation from dust and mold spores; unroll or flake rounds when you can.

Buying Hay Like You Mean It

Buy early. First cutting sells out, and January prices punish procrastinators. Buy from the field if you can — you will see exactly what you are getting and skip the barn's storage markup. Judge quality with your senses: good hay is green (not yellow or brown), soft and pliable (not stemmy), sweet-smelling (never musty — musty means mold, and moldy hay can kill), and leafy with minimal dust. Legume hays (alfalfa, clover) run higher in protein and calcium; grass hays run higher in fiber and lower in calories — match the hay to the horse, not the price tag.

Ask the grower three questions: what cutting is it, has it been rained on, and what do the bales actually weigh. “Rained on” hay is not automatically ruined — a light rain on cured hay is survivable — but heavy rain leaches nutrients and invites mold, and you should pay accordingly. Get a hay analysis ($20–30 at most extension labs) if you are feeding performance horses, broodmares, or easy keepers where every calorie counts; for the average backyard horse, eyes and nose plus the 2% rule get you there.

Storage: Dry Hay Is Money

Hay stored outside uncovered can lose 5–30% of its dry matter to weather — the outside weathers, molds, and gets refused. Inside storage on pallets (airflow underneath matters as much as a roof overhead) preserves both weight and feed value. If you must store outside, get round bales under a tarp or a bale sleeve, ends facing the prevailing weather, on high ground — and budget the losses honestly in your buy calculation.

Stack with the strings/twine intact and cut them at feeding time; stack cut edges out so any moisture runs off rather than wicking in. And rotate: feed oldest first. Hay keeps a year or more if truly dry, but last year's leftover bales are this year's first-fed, not a monument.

Transitioning: Pasture to Hay and Back

The hindgut microbes that ferment fiber need 10–14 days to adjust to a new forage volume or type. Switching a horse from lush pasture to full hay overnight — or from grass hay to rich alfalfa — is asking for colic. Blend the transition: start with mostly the old forage, shift the ratio a little each day, and watch manure (loose means slow down). The same applies in spring, in reverse, and spring grass deserves extra caution: the fructan sugar spike in spring pasture is a laminitis trigger for metabolic horses and ponies. Limit grazing hours, use a grazing muzzle, or keep them on hay until the grass hardens off.

When Hay Is Not Enough

Hay covers maintenance and light work for most horses. It stops being enough when: the horse is in moderate or heavy work (needs concentrated calories), is a hard keeper dropping weight on full hay, is a late-pregnant or lactating mare (protein and mineral demands jump), or is a senior with worn teeth who quids (drops wads of half-chewed hay) — that horse is starving in front of full nets and needs soaked hay cubes, beet pulp, or complete senior feed.

Grain and concentrates are supplements to the forage base, never replacements: the golden rule is forage first, always at least 1.5% of body weight, with concentrates filling the gap. And any ration change for a metabolic, insulin-resistant, or ulcer-prone horse runs through the vet — the 2% rule is a starting point, not a prescription.

Frequently asked questions

How much hay does a horse eat per day?

About 2% of body weight in forage per day for a horse at maintenance - 20 lb for a 1,000 lb horse. Easy keepers do well at 1.5%, while hard keepers, late-pregnant mares, and horses in heavy work need up to 2.5%. These are total forage figures; reduce hay if the horse also gets grain.

How many bales of hay does one horse need for winter?

It depends on the winter length and bale weight. A 1,000 lb horse at 2% over a 120-day winter needs about 2,400 lb before waste - roughly 60 small square bales at 40 lb each, or 2-3 round bales at 1,000 lb. Always weigh your actual bales; 'small squares' range from 35 to 60 lb.

Should I feed more hay in cold weather?

Yes. Below about 18°F, a horse in winter coat needs roughly 2 extra pounds of hay per day for every 10 degrees colder, because fermenting fiber is what generates body heat. Add more hay rather than more grain, and keep a cold-snap buffer of extra bales on hand.

Are round bales or square bales better?

Round bales are much cheaper per ton but need equipment to move and a feeder to avoid waste of up to 57% without one. Small squares cost more per ton but are easy to weigh, portion, and store, with minimal waste. For one or two horses without a tractor, small squares usually win.

How much hay is wasted with different feeders?

Research shows about 5% waste feeding small squares in a hay rack, around 13% feeding on the ground, and 5-33% for round bales in a feeder versus up to 57% with no feeder at all. A good feeder typically pays for itself in a single winter.

Can a horse live on hay alone?

Yes, if the hay is good quality and the horse is at maintenance or in light work - many horses thrive on hay plus a vitamin-mineral supplement. Horses in moderate to heavy work, hard keepers, late-pregnant or nursing mares, and seniors with bad teeth usually need concentrates or complete feeds added to the forage base.
