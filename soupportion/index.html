About this tool

Soup is the most forgiving dish in the kitchen and the easiest to scale badly, because a recipe that serves four cannot simply be doubled by doubling everything in sight. Seasoning, cooking time and pot size all change at different rates, and a soup that tastes perfect for four can taste thin, salty or crowded for twelve. A soup serving calculator solves the quantity half of the problem cleanly, by scaling volume, stock, vegetables and protein to your exact guest count, so you can focus on flavour instead of arithmetic.

The standard serving is about 300 millilitres per person for a main-course soup, which is a generous bowl, not a timid cup. A starter soup needs only about 250 millilitres, because a full meal follows, while a hearty winter soup with bread can stretch to 400 millilitres per person without anyone feeling short-changed. These volumes are the anchor for everything else: multiply servings by serving size and you have the total volume, and every other ingredient follows from that total in predictable proportions.

Stock is the backbone, and it should make up about seventy percent of the total volume, with the rest coming from vegetables, protein and the water those ingredients release as they cook. Four servings of standard soup need 1,200 millilitres in total and about 840 millilitres of stock, which is a sensible amount for a medium pot. If you use a concentrated stock cube or paste, follow the packet dilution carefully, because doubling the cubes without doubling the water is the most common scaling mistake, and it produces a soup that is salty before it is flavourful.

Vegetables scale almost perfectly linearly, at about 120 grams per serving, which means a four-serving soup needs roughly 480 grams of mixed vegetables. This is where scaling feels generous, because a large pot of soup can absorb a whole bag of carrots, a couple of onions and several potatoes without any trouble. Cut vegetables smaller for large batches, because they cook faster and distribute more evenly, and add delicate vegetables like spinach or peas near the end of cooking regardless of batch size, so they stay bright instead of dissolving.

Protein needs about 80 grams per serving, whether it is chicken, lentils, beans or sausage, which gives a four-serving soup about 320 grams in total. Dried lentils and beans deserve special care when scaling, because they absorb water and swell, so a soup that looks right at the start can become a thick stew by the end. Add extra stock for legume soups and keep a jug of hot stock nearby for thinning, because a soup that is too thick is easy to fix and a soup that is too thin is hard to rescue without diluting flavour.

Pot size is the practical constraint that ruins more large soups than any recipe error. Your pot must hold at least twenty percent more than the total soup volume, because soup bubbles, splashes and needs room for stirring, and a pot filled to the brim will boil over at the worst moment. A 1.2 litre soup needs at least a 1.5 litre pot in theory and a 2 litre pot in practice, while a 3.6 litre batch for twelve needs a 5 litre stockpot. If your largest pot is too small, split the batch across two pots rather than cramming it into one, because two well-cooked pots beat one overflowing disaster.

Seasoning does not scale linearly, and this is the secret that separates good soup cooks from frustrated ones. Salt, chilli, acid and herbs should be scaled to about eighty percent of the linear figure at first, then adjusted by tasting, because flavours concentrate as soup simmers and reduces. A soup that serves twelve needs more salt than a soup that serves four, but not three times as much, and the same applies to stock cubes, tomato paste and spice. Taste at the end, season in stages, and remember that a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar at the finish brightens a large pot more safely than extra salt.

Cooking time changes with batch size in a gentle way. A larger pot takes longer to come to a boil, because there is more cold mass to heat, but once simmering, the cooking time for vegetables and protein stays roughly the same, with perhaps ten to fifteen extra minutes for a very large batch. Do not rush a large soup by turning the heat high, because vigorous boiling breaks vegetables apart and clouds the broth, while a gentle simmer keeps everything distinct and clear. Patience is the ingredient that scaling cannot replace.

Leftovers are soup's greatest virtue, and scaling with leftovers in mind is smart rather than wasteful. Most soups keep for three to four days in the fridge and freeze beautifully for up to three months, which means cooking for eight when you need four gives you a free meal later in the week. Cool soup quickly in shallow containers, label the container with the date, and reheat only the portion you need, because repeated reheating dulls flavour and texture. A freezer full of portioned soup is one of the kindest gifts a cook can give their future self.

The comfort of soup comes from its generosity, and generosity starts with knowing how much to make. Enter your servings, choose your bowl size, and let SoupPortion write the scaled recipe for you, from total volume to pot size. Then cook slowly, season carefully, and serve hot with good bread, because a soup that feeds everyone exactly right is a small act of care that tastes like home.

A closing thought on serving, because soup is one of the few dishes where the vessel and the garnish matter almost as much as the soup itself. Warm the bowls before ladling, because cold bowls steal heat from hot soup in seconds, and a warm bowl keeps the soup at eating temperature while guests settle. Garnish simply and at the last moment: a swirl of cream, a scattering of fresh herbs, a handful of crunchy croutons or toasted seeds, each adding contrast in colour, texture or temperature. Serve bread on the side rather than in the soup, so it stays crisp for dipping, and keep a jug of hot stock nearby for guests who like their soup thinner. These finishing touches take two minutes and transform a well-scaled soup into a meal that feels considered rather than merely cooked.

Another scaling subtlety concerns noodles, rice and pasta added to soup, because these starches absorb liquid and expand dramatically, and a soup that is perfectly balanced at serving time can become a solid mass by the second helping. If you plan to add noodles or rice, cook them separately and add them to individual bowls rather than to the pot, because this keeps the soup's liquid ratio stable and lets leftovers reheat without turning to porridge. If you must add starch to the pot, increase the stock by about twenty percent and add the starch near the end of cooking, tasting frequently, because the window between perfectly cooked and overcooked noodles in hot soup is only a few minutes. For batch cooking, freeze the soup without starch and cook fresh noodles when reheating, which gives you the best of both worlds: convenient leftovers and perfect texture every time.

Frequently asked questions

How much soup per person?

About 300 millilitres for a main-course bowl, 250 millilitres for a starter, and 400 millilitres for a hearty winter serving.

How much stock do I need for soup?

Stock should make up about 70 percent of the total soup volume, with the rest from vegetables, protein and cooking liquid.

What size pot do I need for soup?

Your pot must hold at least 20 percent more than the total soup volume, so a 1.2 litre soup needs a 2 litre pot in practice.

Does seasoning scale linearly when doubling soup?

No, season to about 80 percent of the linear figure first, then taste and adjust, because flavours concentrate as soup simmers.

How long does soup keep in the fridge?

Most soups keep for three to four days in the fridge and up to three months in the freezer in portioned containers.

How much protein per serving of soup?

About 80 grams per serving, whether it is chicken, lentils, beans or sausage.
