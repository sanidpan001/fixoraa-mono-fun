What Is a Board Foot, Really?

A board foot is a unit of volume, not length — and that single fact clears up most of the confusion around lumber pricing. One board foot equals 144 cubic inches of wood: imagine a board 12 inches wide, 12 inches long, and 1 inch thick. It doesn't matter how those dimensions are arranged — a long thin strip and a short fat plank can both be exactly one board foot as long as their volume matches.

Hardwood lumber in North America is priced by the board foot because it's the only unit that accounts for thickness as well as length and width. Linear feet ignore thickness entirely (fine for trim sold by length, useless for comparing a 4/4 board against an 8/4 board). Square feet ignore thickness too (fine for flooring coverage, misleading for cost). When a sawyer quotes $6.50 per board foot of black walnut, the board-foot math is what lets you compare that quote against another yard's $7.00 — apples to apples, regardless of how each yard cuts its stock.

The Board Foot Formula

There are two equivalent forms, depending on which units you measure in:

Board feet = thickness (in) × width (in) × length (ft) ÷ 12

Board feet = thickness (in) × width (in) × length (in) ÷ 144

A worked example: one board, 8 feet long, 6 inches wide, 1 inch thick. Board feet = (1 × 6 × 8) ÷ 12 = 4 board feet. Buying ten of them? Multiply by ten: 40 board feet. At $5.00 per board foot, that's $200 of lumber — before waste.

This calculator also reports the companion units every woodworker needs: linear feet (just the length — what trim and molding are sold by), square feet (width × length — coverage for panels, tabletops, and flooring), and cubic feet (board feet ÷ 12 — total solid volume, handy for weight and shipping estimates).

The Quarter System: Reading Hardwood Thickness

Walk into a hardwood dealer and you'll hear boards described as "four-quarter" or "eight-quarter." The quarter system counts quarters of an inch of rough-sawn thickness:

4/41 inch3/4 inGeneral furniture, cabinetry
5/41¼ inch1 inTable tops, stair treads
6/41½ inch1¼ inWorkbench tops, thick legs
8/42 inches1¾ inLegs, turning blanks, chunky parts
12/43 inches2¾ inLarge turnings, sculptural work
16/44 inches3¾ inMantle beams, massive legs

Two subtleties matter. First, quarter-system thickness refers to the rough board; surfacing (planing smooth) removes about 1/4 inch total, which is why 4/4 stock finishes at 3/4 inch. Second — and this is where beginners lose money — board feet are calculated on the rough/nominal dimensions, not the surfaced ones. You pay for the wood the mill had to grow, dry, and saw, including what the planer later removes. This calculator's quarter picker uses the correct nominal values.

Board Foot vs Linear Foot vs Square Foot

Use the right unit for the right purchase, or you'll misprice the project:

  • Board feet — buying hardwood lumber for furniture. The universal pricing unit at hardwood dealers and sawmills.
  • Linear feet — buying trim, molding, baseboard, or dimensional 2×4s sold by length. Thickness is standardized, so length is all that varies.
  • Square feet — buying flooring, paneling, or sheet goods; or estimating how much surface your boards will cover.

The classic mix-up: "how many linear feet do I need to cover this wall?" Barnboard and shiplap sellers hear this weekly. The answer needs board width — a 6-inch-wide board covers half the wall per linear foot that a 12-inch board does. Convert to square feet first (wall area ÷ board width), then to linear feet if that's how the product is sold.

How to Measure a Board Correctly

  1. Length in feet — the long dimension. Hardwood is sold in even lengths (6, 8, 10, 12 ft); measure to the nearest inch and convert.
  2. Width in inches — the face width at the narrowest point. Rough hardwood boards taper and wane; dealers measure the narrowest usable width, and so should you.
  3. Thickness — in quarters (4/4, 8/4…) for hardwood, or actual inches for surfaced stock. Use the nominal rough dimension for board-foot pricing.
  4. Multiply and divide by 12 — then multiply by the number of identical boards.

Measure every board you intend to buy, not just one: a "6-inch" stack of rough lumber routinely spans 5½ to 7 inches board to board. Waste: The 10–15% Nobody Budgets

Rough lumber is not a finished part. Between the saw and your project you lose wood to: surfacing (planing rough faces flat), defects (knots, checks, wane, sticker stain — you cut around them), grain matching (resawing and book-matching eat width), and mistakes (every shop has a story). The professional rule is to buy 10–15% more board footage than the finished parts require — 15–20% for highly figured stock or first-time projects in an unfamiliar species.

The waste control in this calculator applies your chosen percentage to the measured total and reports the buy quantity — the number to actually order. On a $400 walnut order, 15% waste is $60 of insurance against coming up one board short mid-project, when the yard's next flitch won't match your grain anyway.

Pricing: Reading a Hardwood Quote

Hardwood price lists quote dollars per board foot, and the spread between species is dramatic: poplar and soft maple at the budget end, black walnut and white oak commanding premiums, exotics higher still. Three things move a quote besides species: thickness (8/4 costs more per board foot than 4/4 — thicker clear stock is rarer), grade (FAS/F1F selects vs #1 Common — higher grades yield more clear cuttings per board), and surfacing (S2S/S4S surfaced stock costs more per board foot than rough).

Always compare quotes in dollars per board foot for the same thickness and surfacing. A $5.50/BF quote for rough 4/4 cherry and a $6.25/BF quote for surfaced 4/4 cherry may describe nearly the same wood — the second yard just did the planing for you. A Full Project Example

Say you're building a shaker-style side table needing: a top from two 8/4 boards (2 in × 8 in × 4 ft), four legs from one 8/4 board (2 in × 6 in × 8 ft), and an apron set from 4/4 stock (1 in × 5 in × 8 ft, two boards). The math: top boards: (2 × 8 × 4) ÷ 12 = 5.33 BF each → 10.67 BF. Leg board: (2 × 6 × 8) ÷ 12 = 8 BF. Aprons: (1 × 5 × 8) ÷ 12 = 3.33 BF each → 6.67 BF. Finished-parts total: 25.34 BF. Add 15% waste: 29.14 BF to buy. At $6.00/BF cherry, the lumber budget is $174.84. That is the entire workflow this page automates: dimensions in, buy quantity and budget out.

Reading a Board: Grain, Defects, and What They Cost You

Board footage tells you how much wood you're paying for, but it says nothing about how much of that wood becomes furniture. Learning to read a board at the yard is the skill that separates cheap projects from expensive ones. Start with the faces: flat-sawn boards show cathedral grain patterns and are the most common and affordable; quarter-sawn boards show straight, parallel grain lines, move less with humidity, and typically cost 15–30% more per board foot. Rift-sawn sits between the two. For table tops and wide panels, quarter-sawn stock is worth the premium — it stays flatter across seasons, which means less planing, less waste, and fewer callbacks on client work.

Next, scan for defects you'll cut around — and remember you pay board-foot price for every inch you discard. Wane (bark or missing wood along an edge) is the most common; a board with heavy wane on both edges can lose a full inch of usable width, which quietly inflates your cost per usable board foot. Checks are end cracks from drying; expect to trim 2–4 inches off each end of rough stock, more on bargain piles. Knots aren't automatically waste — a tight, small knot can be a feature in rustic work — but loose or black knots near a joint are. Sticker stain (dark bands where drying stickers sat) usually planes out, but on thin 4/4 stock it can survive surfacing and ruin a finish.

Finally, sight down the board's length like a rifle barrel. Bow (curve along the length), cup (curve across the width), twist (one corner lifted), and crook (curve along the edge) all steal thickness when you flatten the board. A twisted 8/4 board might surface down to barely 6/4 of usable thickness — you've paid 8/4 price for 6/4 wood. Slight bow in a long board is manageable; twist is the dealbreaker. Chalk-mark the worst defects right at the yard so you remember which boards need the most trimming when you build the cut list at home. The calculator's waste percentage exists precisely for this reality: honest defect assessment at the pile is what turns the 10–15% rule from a guess into a budget.

Buying Smart at the Lumber Yard

Bring your cut list, a tape measure, and chalk. Sight down each board for twist and bow — a twisted board surfs into far less usable wood than its board footage suggests. Check the ends for checks (cracks) that will be cut off, and remember you pay board-foot price for those waste inches too. For small projects, ask about shorts and offcuts: many yards sell short lengths at a discount, and a cutting board or box project fits beautifully in stock the furniture makers passed over. Finally, let lumber acclimate in your shop for a week or two before milling; wood moves with humidity, and parts milled from unacclimated stock move after assembly.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate board feet?

Multiply thickness (inches) × width (inches) × length (feet), then divide by 12. Example: a 1 in × 6 in × 8 ft board = (1 × 6 × 8) ÷ 12 = 4 board feet. Multiply by the number of identical boards for the total.

What does 4/4 mean in lumber?

4/4 (four-quarter) means 1 inch of rough-sawn thickness. The quarter system counts quarters of an inch: 8/4 is 2 inches, 12/4 is 3 inches. Note that 4/4 rough stock surfaces down to about 3/4 inch — but board feet are always calculated on the rough dimension.

What is the difference between a board foot and a linear foot?

A board foot measures volume (144 cubic inches) and accounts for thickness; a linear foot measures length only. Hardwood is priced by the board foot because thickness varies, while trim and molding are sold by the linear foot because their cross-section is standardized.

How much extra lumber should I buy for waste?

Buy 10–15% more board footage than your finished parts need — 15–20% for figured stock or unfamiliar species. Waste comes from planing rough faces flat, cutting around knots and defects, grain matching, and the occasional mistake.

Is the board foot formula the same for hardwood and softwood?

Yes — the formula is identical for hardwoods, softwoods, and exotics. The difference is commercial: hardwoods are typically sold by the board foot, while construction softwoods are usually sold by the linear foot or per piece.

Do I calculate board feet before or after planing?

Dealers price the rough/nominal dimensions, not the surfaced size — so calculate with the quarter-system (rough) thickness. You pay for the wood the mill sawed, including what the planer later removes.
