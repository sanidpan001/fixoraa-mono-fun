    Four Party Modes in One Box

    Most party game sites give you one game and call it a day. Darely packs four distinct modes into a single page, so the energy never flatlines. When one game runs its course, switching takes a single tap.

    Truth or Dare

    The undisputed king of party games for adults and family gatherings alike. One hundred original prompts — forty family-safe truths and dares, thirty-five cheeky all-around ones, and twenty-five wild cards for brave groups. In this mode you get dedicated TRUTH and DARE buttons, so each player genuinely chooses their fate. Truths range from lighthearted ("What is your favorite childhood cartoon?") to deliciously revealing ("What is the most embarrassing thing on your camera roll right now?"), while dares scale from silly physical challenges to full-room performances.

    Would You Rather

    The perfect debate starter. One hundred would you rather questions force impossible choices — fly or be invisible, unlimited money or unlimited time, fight one horse-sized duck or one hundred duck-sized horses. There are no wrong answers, which makes this mode ideal for mixed groups where not everyone wants to perform a dare. It reliably produces the loudest arguments and the funniest justifications of any mode in the box.

    Never Have I Ever

    The confession game. One hundred "Never have I ever..." statements, from innocent ("Never have I ever ridden a bicycle") to adventurous ("Never have I ever taken a spontaneous road trip"). Play it classic-style with fingers up, or use it as a storytelling prompt — whoever has done it tells the tale. It is the fastest way to discover which of your friends has secretly lived three lives.

    Party Mix

    Cannot decide? Party Mix shuffles all 400 prompts into one unpredictable deck. A heartfelt truth can be followed by a ridiculous dare, then a would-you-rather debate. It is the mode for groups that get bored easily and want maximum chaos per minute.

    How to Play: Rules for Each Mode

    Truth or Dare rules

    Gather in a circle and decide the turn order — or use Darely's built-in pass-and-play rotation to do it for you. The current player chooses Truth or Dare and taps the matching button. Answer honestly or complete the dare. Refuse, and the forfeit tracker logs your debt: a penalty the group agrees on, like buying the next round of snacks or doing the dishes. Award points to teams for completed dares to raise the stakes.

    Would You Rather rules

    Read the card aloud. Everyone picks a side and defends their choice — the best justification wins the round. For team play, the minority side loses a point, or the team with the funniest defense steals one. There is no timer unless you want one; the debates are the game.

    Never Have I Ever rules

    Everyone holds up five (or ten) fingers. Read each statement aloud; anyone who HAS done it puts a finger down and, if the group demands it, tells the story. Last person with fingers up wins. With teams, tally total fingers lost per team across ten cards — the team with the most "guilty" fingers takes the round, or the fewest, depending on how you frame it.

    Team-vs-Team Scoreboard

    Solo party games are fun; team party games are unforgettable. Darely includes a built-in scoreboard for up to bragging-rights warfare between two sides of the room. Rename the teams (couples versus singles, hosts versus guests, left couch versus right couch), then award points for completed dares, winning debates and surviving never-have-I-ever rounds. The live leader banner keeps the rivalry visible all night, and one tap resets everything for a rematch. Because scoring is manual and flexible, you can invent your own house rules — double points for wild-level dares, bonus points for the funniest forfeit, sudden-death tiebreakers with Party Mix cards.

    Pass-and-Play Rotation and Forfeit Tracker

    Two small features that solve the two biggest party-game headaches. Pass-and-play rotation means you add every player's name once, then tap "Next player" to rotate turns fairly — no arguments about whose turn it is, no quiet players getting skipped, no loud players hogging the spotlight. The current player's name appears on the prompt card itself, so everyone knows who is in the hot seat even after the phone has been passed around the circle three times.

    The forfeit tracker handles the classic dare-dodgers. When someone chickens out, log their forfeit with one tap — the tracker records who owes what and counts repeat offenders. Settle up at the end of the night, or let the group vote on whether a forfeit was properly served. It keeps the game honest without killing the mood, and the running tally of forfeits becomes its own comedy highlight reel.

    Hosting Tips: Running a Legendary Game Night

    A great game night is 20 percent prompts and 80 percent hosting. Start with the spiciness slider one notch lower than you think you need — it is easy to turn up, and an early awkward card can stall the room. Use Party Mix for the first fifteen minutes while people arrive; the variety warms up shy players. Once the group is loose, switch to dedicated Truth or Dare for peak energy, and save Never Have I Ever for later in the night when stories flow freely.

    Keep teams balanced by mixing bold and shy players on each side, and let the losing team pick the first card of every new round — it keeps them invested. Use the forfeit tracker generously but kindly: forfeits should be funny, never humiliating. The golden rule of party games for adults is that anyone can pass, no questions asked, with the only cost being a logged forfeit. Safety first with physical dares — if a card does not suit your space or your group, deal the next one; with 400 prompts and automatic reshuffling, you will never run dry.

    Finally, put one phone in the middle and let it be the game box. Darely's no-repeat dealing means every card feels fresh, and when the deck runs out it reshuffles automatically so the party never stalls. Between the scoreboard rivalries, the forfeit debts and your custom packs, do not be surprised when "one more round" turns into one more hour.
