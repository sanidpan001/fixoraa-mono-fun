The complete guide to planning a sourdough bake day

Why sourdough needs a schedule planner, not a recipe

Most sourdough recipes read like a story: feed your starter, wait, mix, wait, fold, wait, shape, wait, bake. What they rarely tell you is when — and "when" is everything in sourdough. A recipe that works beautifully in a 24°C kitchen in Lisbon can collapse into a soupy mess in a 29°C kitchen in Mumbai, or turn into an under-proofed brick in a 17°C kitchen in winter. This is exactly the problem a sourdough schedule planner solves. Instead of fixed times, it gives you a plan anchored to two things you actually control: the moment you want bread out of the oven, and the temperature of your kitchen.

CrumbClock is a sourdough timeline calculator built around that idea. You enter your target bake time — say Saturday at 9 AM — plus your kitchen temperature, and it generates a complete backwards timeline: when to feed your levain, when to autolyse, when to mix, when each set of folds happens, how long bulk fermentation should run, when to shape, how long to proof, and when to preheat the oven. Every fermentation step stretches or shrinks with temperature, because fermentation is chemistry, and chemistry obeys temperature.

How backwards planning works

Professional bakers never plan forwards from "now" — they plan backwards from the bake. A bakery that needs 200 loaves ready at 7 AM does not start at some arbitrary morning hour and hope; it subtracts proof time, shaping time, bulk time, mix time and levain-build time from 7 AM until it arrives at the moment the first preferment must be fed, which might be two days earlier. CrumbClock applies the same professional method to home baking. The target bake time is the fixed point, and every step is subtracted from it in order.

This matters because sourdough has hard edges: an over-proofed loaf cannot be un-proofed, and a levain that peaked four hours before you mixed is a levain past its prime. When you plan backwards, the high-stakes moments — the levain feed and the mix — land at times you chose deliberately rather than times you stumbled into. If the backwards plan says "feed levain at 11 PM Friday," you know before you start that this bake requires a late night, and you can either accept it or flip on the cold-retard option and move the whole proof into the fridge overnight.

Temperature: the hidden variable in every recipe

Wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria — the two workforces in your starter — are temperature-sensitive organisms. As a rule of thumb borrowed from food science, their activity roughly doubles for every 10°C rise in temperature. That means a bulk fermentation timed at 5 hours in a 24°C kitchen wants about 6.6 hours at 20°C, about 7.6 hours at 18°C, and only about 3.8 hours at 28°C. The difference between 20°C and 28°C is nearly a factor of two. No fixed-time recipe survives that spread, which is why "my crumb is dense" and "my dough over-proofed" are the two most common complaints in sourdough forums — and both are temperature problems wearing recipe costumes.

CrumbClock's temperature curve applies the same doubling rule to every temperature-sensitive step: bulk fermentation, final proof and the levain build. Slide the kitchen temperature control and watch the whole timeline breathe — colder kitchens push the levain feed earlier into the previous evening, warmer kitchens compress everything into a single day. This is the single biggest upgrade you can make to your baking: replacing cookbook times with your-kitchen times. A thermometer on the counter is the cheapest piece of sourdough equipment you will ever buy.

The levain build: timing your starter's peak

Your levain — the offshoot of starter you build specifically for one bake — is at its best for a short window: when it has roughly doubled or tripled, domes on top, and passes the float test. Mix too early and the yeast population is still ramping up; mix too late and the acids have taken over, which degrades gluten and gives you a sour, slack loaf. The peak arrives a predictable number of hours after feeding, and that number depends on two things: the feed ratio and the temperature.

A 1:1:1 feed (equal parts starter, flour, water) peaks fast — around 4 to 5 hours at room temperature. A classic 1:2:2 feed takes about 7 hours. A stiff 1:5:5 feed can take 10 to 12 hours, which makes it perfect for an overnight build: feed before bed, mix in the morning. CrumbClock's levain-build calculator combines your chosen feed ratio with your kitchen temperature to compute both the exact gram weights (starter, flour, water) and the exact feed time, so the peak lands precisely at mix time. It also scales the levain to your dough: about 20% of flour weight for a country loaf, a little less for whole-grain and pizza doughs, a little more for enriched doughs.

Whole grains drink more water: the blend adjuster

Swap half the white flour in a recipe for whole wheat, keep everything else the same, and you will get a stiff, dry, tearing dough. Whole-grain flours contain bran and germ, which absorb significantly more water than white flour's mostly-endosperm makeup. Rye is even thirstier, and its pentosans create a sticky, gel-like dough that behaves nothing like wheat. This is why whole-grain recipes fail when bakers reuse white-flour hydrations — the dough was never actually at the hydration the recipe claimed, in any meaningful handling sense.

The whole-grain blend adjuster in CrumbClock translates your blend into a hydration target that will handle like your baseline. Roughly: every 25% of whole wheat in the blend wants about 3.5 extra points of hydration; rye wants about 4.5 points per 25%; spelt, being more delicate, wants about 1 point per 25%. A 50/50 white and whole wheat blend at a 70% baseline therefore lands near 77% — and the tool will tell you so, with a note explaining the shift. The planner then computes water weight from the adjusted hydration, so your recipe stays consistent even as you experiment with blends. Start with the suggestion, then tune by feel: if the dough tears during folds, add water next time; if it spreads flat, pull back.

Baker's percentages: the universal language of dough

Every professional formula in the world is written in baker's percentages, and once you learn them you will never read a recipe the old way again. The rule is simple: total flour weight is always 100%, and every other ingredient is expressed as a percentage of it. A dough with 500 g flour, 350 g water, 100 g levain and 10 g salt is 70% hydration, 20% levain, 2% salt — whether you are making one loaf or forty. Percentages let you scale any recipe to any flour weight in seconds, compare two recipes at a glance, and diagnose problems ("ah, this recipe is only 62% hydration — no wonder it's stiff").

CrumbClock includes a built-in baker's percentage calculator that derives the full formula from your inputs: hydration, levain and salt as percentages of flour, the flour-component split across your blend, and the total dough weight. Use it as a sanity check before you commit: if the hydration looks far from what you intended, adjust the blend or the water before you mix, not after. Over time, thinking in percentages is what turns recipe-following into recipe-designing — the moment sourdough stops being intimidating and starts being play.

Cold retard: flavor, scheduling freedom, and easier scoring

Retarding — proofing the shaped loaf in the fridge at 3–5°C for 8 to 16 hours — is the home baker's superpower, and it does three things at once. First, flavor: cold temperatures slow yeast more than they slow acid-producing bacteria, so the dough develops deeper, more complex sourness without over-proofing. Second, scheduling: shaping in the evening and baking straight from the fridge in the morning turns sourdough from a hostage situation into a routine. Third, handling: cold dough is firm dough, and firm dough scores cleanly with a lame instead of dragging and deflating.

One caution: bake straight from the fridge. Letting a retarded loaf warm up on the counter re-accelerates fermentation unevenly and costs you the oven spring you earned overnight. Preheat your Dutch oven fully, turn the cold loaf onto parchment, score decisively, and get it into the heat fast. In CrumbClock, toggling the cold-retard option rebuilds your timeline around a fixed 12-hour fridge proof — watch how it frees up your evening and anchors the bake to a calm morning instead of a frantic afternoon.

Reading your dough, not just your clock

A schedule planner gives you the map; your senses still drive the car. Times in any planner — including this one — are estimates built on averages: your starter's particular vigor, your flour's protein content, and even your water's mineral profile all nudge fermentation one way or the other. So pair the timeline with the classic visual cues. Bulk fermentation is done when the dough has grown roughly 50–75%, shows bubbles on the surface and sides, jiggles like set custard when you shake the bowl, and pulls away with visible gluten strands. The poke test still rules the final proof: press a floured finger in; if the dent springs back slowly and incompletely, bake now.

Keep a bake log for your first few loaves: kitchen temperature, actual bulk time, how the dough looked, and the result. Within three or four bakes you will know your starter's personal tempo, and the planner's estimates will start landing within minutes instead of within an hour. That feedback loop — plan, bake, observe, adjust — is the entire craft of sourdough in one sentence. The planner just makes the first step, the plan, take thirty seconds instead of thirty minutes of anxious arithmetic.

A sample bake day, planned backwards

To make it concrete, here is what a CrumbClock plan looks like for a classic country loaf — 500 g flour, 70% hydration, 20% levain — in a 22°C kitchen, targeting bread out of the oven Saturday at 9 AM with a cold retard. Friday around 1 PM you feed the levain 1:2:2. Friday evening at 8 PM you autolyse flour and water. At 8:30 PM you mix in the levain, then the salt. Four sets of stretch-and-folds carry you to about 10:30 PM while bulk fermentation runs underneath, ending around 2 AM — you are asleep; the dough works the night shift. Saturday at 2 AM would be shaping time, which is absurd, so this is exactly the plan where you would instead shape Friday night and retard: shape at 11 PM, fridge overnight, and Saturday morning is just preheat, score, and bake at 9 AM. The planner shows you these collisions before they happen, which is the whole point: a sourdough schedule planner does not just tell you what to do, it tells you what your plan costs in sleep and timing, while you can still change it.

Whether this is your first loaf or your five-hundredth, the discipline is the same: fix the bake time, respect the temperature, feed the levain on time, and let the dough tell you when it is ready. Sourdough scheduling FAQs

How far in advance should I plan a sourdough bake day?

At least 24 to 36 hours before you want bread out of the oven. A typical country loaf needs 6 to 10 hours of active timeline (levain build, autolyse, mix, bulk fermentation, shaping and proofing) plus an optional overnight cold retard. CrumbClock plans backwards from your target bake time so every step gets an exact clock time.

How does kitchen temperature change bulk fermentation time?

Fermentation speed roughly doubles with every 10°C rise in temperature. A bulk fermentation that takes 5 hours at 24°C can take 8 or more hours at 18°C and under 4 hours at 28°C. CrumbClock applies this temperature curve to bulk, proof and levain-build times so your schedule matches your actual kitchen, not a cookbook's.

What is a good hydration for whole wheat sourdough?

Whole wheat flour absorbs noticeably more water than white bread flour. While a white country loaf works at 70–72% hydration, a 50% whole wheat blend usually needs 77–80% to handle the same way. CrumbClock's blend adjuster adds roughly 14 points of hydration per 100% whole wheat and 18 points per 100% rye, then caps the result at a workable 100%.

How much starter do I need for a loaf of sourdough?

A common starting point is 20% of the flour weight in ripe levain for a country loaf, 15–18% for whole-grain and pizza doughs, and up to 25% for enriched doughs. CrumbClock's levain-build calculator turns that into exact starter, flour and water weights for a 1:2:2 feed and tells you exactly when to feed it so it peaks at mix time.

Should I cold retard my sourdough in the fridge?

Cold retarding for 8 to 16 hours at 3–5°C slows fermentation, develops deeper flavor and sourness, and makes the dough easier to score because it is firm. It is also the easiest way to fit baking into a schedule: shape in the evening, bake straight from the fridge in the morning. Toggle the cold-retard option in CrumbClock to rebuild your timeline around an overnight proof.

What are baker's percentages and why do bakers use them?

Baker's percentages express every ingredient as a percentage of the total flour weight, which is always 100%. They let you scale any recipe up or down and compare formulas instantly: 70% hydration means 700 grams of water per 1000 grams of flour. CrumbClock computes them automatically from your recipe so you can sanity-check and scale with confidence.
