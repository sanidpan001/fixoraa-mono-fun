What Solunar Theory Actually Claims

In 1926, John Alden Knight collected fishing records and noticed a pattern: fish seemed most active when the moon was directly overhead or underfoot. He called these peaks major periods, each lasting about two hours, with shorter minor periods at moonrise and moonset. The idea — that the moon’s position influences animal feeding behavior — became solunar theory, and it has organized fishing calendars ever since.

The proposed mechanism is part gravitational, part light. The moon’s pull drives tides in salt water, and many species time feeding to tidal movement. Moonlight changes nighttime visibility for predators and prey alike. Even in fresh water, where tides are negligible, the light cycle still shifts insect hatches and baitfish behavior, which is why the theory persists far from the ocean.

This page computes those periods for any date from the moon’s phase and position — no account, no app, just the windows. Treat them as a scheduling aid, not a guarantee: the moon suggests, the weather decides.

Major and Minor Periods, Explained

Each day has two major periods of about two hours: one centered on moon transit (the moon at its highest point) and one centered roughly twelve hours later, when the moon is underfoot on the opposite side of the earth. Anglers consistently report these as the strongest bite windows of the day.

Minor periods center on moonrise and moonset and run about an hour each. They are weaker but real — many a slow day has been saved by the minor window. When a major period coincides with sunrise or sunset, old-timers call it the golden overlap, and it is worth planning a trip around.

The timeline above lays all four windows across 24 hours so you can see at a glance whether they fall in fishable daylight or the middle of the night. A "great" solunar day with both majors at 2 a.m. is less useful than an average day with a major at dawn.

Reading the Moon Phase

Solunar tradition holds that the new and full moons produce the strongest activity — the combined gravitational pull of sun and moon is greatest then, and the spring tides that result move the most water. The quarter moons are considered the weakest days of the cycle. The bite score on this page reflects that: it peaks near new and full moons and dips at the quarters.

But phase cuts both ways. A bright full moon can make night-feeding predators so successful after dark that they rest through the morning — which is why some guides quietly prefer the new moon, when daytime feeding stays strong. If you fish mostly at night, the full moon’s light is a genuine advantage for sight-feeding species.

Watch the trend, not just the day: the three days leading into a new or full moon are traditionally rated better than the three days after. Plan multi-day trips on the building side of the peak.

How to Use Today’s Windows

Start with the major windows and build your day around them. If a major runs 9:30–11:30 a.m., be on your spot, rigged and quiet, by 9:00 — the first and last thirty minutes of each window are often the hottest. Use minor windows as your second sessions or as the excuse for "one more cast" that actually has theory behind it.

Match tactics to the window. During majors, fish your confidence baits aggressively and cover water; active fish reward active presentations. During the in-between hours, slow down, downsize, and pick apart cover — or take lunch. Some of the best anglers treat the gaps between windows as rest time so they are sharp when the next window opens.

Log your results against the forecast for a season. Your home water has its own personality — current, clarity, pressure — and your log will teach you how much the solunar windows matter there, which beats any generic rating.

Why Solunar Isn’t Magic (Honest Limits)

Let us be straight: the scientific evidence for solunar effects on freshwater fish is thin. Peer-reviewed studies find weak or inconsistent correlations, and plenty of banner days happen on "poor" rated dates. What the theory reliably does is get you fishing during dawn and dusk more often — because majors frequently land near them — and dawn and dusk are simply the best fishing hours for light-related reasons.

There is also a strong confirmation bias at work: you remember the full-moon night that produced, and forget the full-moon skunk. That does not make the tables useless — a scheduling framework that puts you on the water at good hours, consistently, has real value even if the mechanism is mostly folklore.

Use the forecast as a tiebreaker, not a religion. A "poor" day with stable weather and the right tide will outfish an "excellent" day with a cold front every time.

Tides vs Solunar: Saltwater Notes

In salt water, tide beats moon position. Moving water concentrates bait and triggers feeding; a slack tide during a major period is still a slack tide. The best saltwater days stack a major window on top of a moving tide — ideally the last two hours of the incoming or the first of the outgoing.

New and full moons bring spring tides: bigger swings, stronger currents. That can be fantastic around structure and passes, but it can also make some spots unfishable. Quarter moons bring gentle neap tides — less dramatic, but comfortable and predictable.

If you fish tidal water, check the tide chart first and the solunar table second. In fresh water, reverse the priority: the solunar windows are the main event, modified by weather and water conditions.

Weather Trumps the Moon

A stable high-pressure system with light winds will make an "average" solunar day fish well; a fast-moving cold front will shut down an "excellent" one. The hierarchy most experienced anglers use: weather first, water conditions second, moon third. Never cancel a trip over a poor moon rating, and never expect the moon to save a terrible forecast.

The two weather details that matter most are barometric trend (steady or slowly falling is good; crashing is bad) and wind (a light chop breaks up the surface and helps predators; a gale makes boat control impossible). Water temperature trends matter too — warming water in spring turns fish on, cooling water in fall concentrates them.

So here is the practical workflow: pick your fishing days by weather and your schedule, then use this page to choose your hours within those days. That is the moon’s proper job — fine-tuning, not deciding.

Planning a Full Fishing Day

Put it together: the night before, pull up the forecast and note the majors and minors. Plan to arrive 30 minutes before the best daylight window. Rig two rods the evening before so the window is not spent tying knots. Pack lunch to eat during the dead hours between windows, not during them.

If a major lands at an awkward hour — say 1–3 p.m. in summer heat — fish shade, deeper water, or moving current during it rather than skipping it. The window still matters; you just have to meet the fish where the conditions are tolerable.

Finally, keep the log. Date, windows fished, weather, water temp, what worked. After twenty trips you will have a personalized solunar table for your water — and that is worth more than any generic calendar, this one included.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best fishing times today?

The two major feeding windows — roughly two hours each around moon transit and moon underfoot — are traditionally the best bites of the day, with shorter minor windows at moonrise and moonset. Enter any date above to see today’s exact windows.

What is a solunar table?

A calendar of predicted peak fish-activity periods based on the moon’s position: major periods when the moon is overhead or underfoot, minor periods at moonrise and moonset. It was developed by John Alden Knight in 1926 from anglers’ catch records.

Is fishing better on a full moon?

Traditionally yes — new and full moons rate highest because the combined sun-moon pull is strongest. But a bright full moon can shift feeding to nighttime, so daytime bites may actually be better around the new moon. Stable weather matters more than phase.

What is the difference between major and minor periods?

Major periods last about two hours each (moon overhead and underfoot) and are considered the strongest bite windows. Minor periods last about an hour each at moonrise and moonset and are weaker but still worth fishing.

Do solunar tables work for freshwater fishing?

They are most defensible in salt water, where the moon drives tides. In fresh water the evidence is weaker, but the tables still tend to put you on the water at dawn and dusk — genuinely the best hours — so they work as a scheduling aid.

Should I trust the moon or the weather forecast?

The weather, every time. A stable-weather day rated "poor" will outfish a cold-front day rated "excellent." Use solunar windows to pick your hours within days chosen for weather and tides.
