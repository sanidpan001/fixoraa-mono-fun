What NPK numbers actually mean

Every fertilizer bag carries three numbers — N-P-K — representing the percentage by weight of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (as P₂O₅) and potassium (as K₂O). A bag labelled 20-10-10 contains 20% nitrogen, 10% phosphate and 10% potash. The rest is filler and carrier. This is why two bags can look similar but dose very differently: 250 g of a 20% nitrogen fertilizer delivers the same nitrogen as 500 g of a 10% one. Always dose by the nutrient, not by the product weight.

The core formula

Grams of product = (target nutrient grams per m² × area in m²) ÷ (NPK percentage ÷ 100). Example: your lawn needs 5 g of nitrogen per m², the area is 10 m², and your fertilizer is 20% nitrogen → (5 × 10) ÷ 0.20 = 250 g of product. That is the entire maths of fertilizing. Everything else — spreader settings, liquid dilution, schedules — is logistics around this number.

Granular vs liquid fertilizer

Granular fertilizer is cheap, slow to moderately fast acting, and ideal for lawns and beds — but it must be spread evenly and watered in, or it burns patches. Liquid fertilizer acts within days and doses precisely, perfect for houseplants and foliar feeding, but it leaches faster so you feed more often. Slow-release granules (often polymer-coated) feed for 2–6 months and are the most forgiving for beginners: one application per season, minimal burn risk.

Liquid dilution made simple

Liquid concentrates list their NPK too. To make a watering-can mix: decide the nitrogen dose (say 0.5 g N per litre for houseplants), then divide by the product's nitrogen fraction. A 10% N liquid needs 5 ml-equivalent per litre... precisely: 0.5 ÷ 0.10 = 5 g of product per litre of water (roughly 5 ml for most liquids). Always add concentrate to water, never the reverse, and apply to moist soil — never to dry roots, which burn easily.

Reading the plant: deficiency signs

  • Nitrogen: uniform yellowing of older, lower leaves; stunted growth.
  • Phosphorus: purplish tints, poor flowering and root growth.
  • Potassium: brown scorched leaf edges, weak stems.
  • Iron: yellowing between veins on new leaves (common in alkaline soils).

Confirm with a soil test before treating — over-fertilizing based on a guess causes more damage than the deficiency.

Avoiding fertilizer burn

Burn happens when fertilizer salts draw water out of roots or leaf tissue. The rules: never exceed the label rate, water granular fertilizer in immediately, do not fertilize drought-stressed plants, keep granules off wet foliage, and halve the dose for seedlings and new transplants. If you over-apply, flood the area with water to leach excess salts away. Slow-release products and organic options (compost, well-rotted manure) are far more forgiving because nutrients release gradually.

Organic vs synthetic

Synthetic fertilizers deliver exact, fast nutrients — ideal when a soil test shows a specific shortfall. Organics (compost, manure, bone meal, seaweed) feed soil biology as well as plants, improve structure, and rarely burn — but their NPK is low and imprecise (compost is roughly 1-1-1). Many gardeners use both: compost as the foundation, synthetics as targeted corrections.

Dose by the nutrient, water it in, feed on schedule — and let the NPK maths do the rest.

Soil testing: the $20 decision-maker

Guessing fertilizer needs is the most expensive mistake in gardening — wrong guesses waste product and damage plants. A basic soil test ($15–30 from agricultural extensions or garden centres) reports pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and organic matter, with exact amendment recommendations. Test every 2–3 years, or before any major planting. The results often surprise: many gardens are already high in phosphorus from years of balanced fertilizer, and adding more only pollutes runoff. Test first, then dose only what is missing.

The micronutrients that matter

Beyond NPK, plants need iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum in tiny amounts. Deficiencies show distinct signatures: iron chlorosis (yellow new leaves, green veins) in alkaline soils; boron causing hollow stems in brassicas; zinc stunting in corn. Most quality fertilizers include a micronutrient package — check the label's fine print. Container plants are most vulnerable since potting mix has no mineral reserves; a complete liquid feed with trace elements prevents most issues.

Feeding a new lawn from seed

New seed needs phosphorus for root establishment — use a starter fertilizer (high middle number, e.g. 10-18-10) at seeding, roughly 5 g N/m² equivalent. Keep the seedbed consistently moist until germination; fertilizer without water burns tender seedlings. Once the grass is 3 inches tall and mowed twice, switch to a regular high-nitrogen program. Avoid weed-and-feed products on new lawns — the herbicide component damages seedlings. Patience beats product: a well-watered new lawn with modest feeding outperforms an over-fertilized one.

Foliar feeding: spraying leaves

Plants absorb nutrients through leaves as well as roots, and foliar feeding delivers corrections in days rather than weeks — ideal for iron chlorosis or a mid-season boost. Mix at half the soil-drench strength to avoid leaf burn, spray in the cool of evening (stomata open, evaporation slow), and coat leaf undersides where most stomata live. Foliar feeding supplements but never replaces soil feeding: leaves cannot take up the volumes roots can. Think of it as an IV drip versus a meal — fast, targeted, and temporary.

Compost tea and organic boosters

Compost tea — compost steeped in aerated water for 24–36 hours — delivers biology more than nutrition: beneficial microbes that colonise leaf surfaces and root zones, suppressing some diseases. The nutrient content is low and variable, so treat it as a supplement, not a fertilizer replacement. Seaweed extract is the more reliable organic booster, supplying potassium, trace minerals and natural growth hormones that help transplants establish and stressed plants recover. Neither replaces the NPK maths — they sit on top of a sound feeding program.

Reading fertilizer labels fully

Beyond NPK, labels list derived-from sources, guaranteed analysis of secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulphur) and sometimes chlorine content — high-chloride fertilizers harm chloride-sensitive crops like tobacco, berries and some ornamentals. "Slow-release" percentages tell you how much nitrogen is coated; 50%+ slow-release means one application feeds for months. Watch for filler claims like "contains biosolids" if you garden organically. The label is a legal document — the guaranteed analysis is the most trustworthy text on the bag.

Calibrating a broadcast spreader

Even the perfect calculated dose fails through an uncalibrated spreader. The catch-pan method: mark a 100 sq ft test area, load a weighed amount, spread at your normal pace, then weigh what remains — the difference over the test area scales to your per-1000-sq-ft rate. Adjust the gate and repeat until it matches your target. Walk at a steady pace with slight overlap between passes; most striping complaints trace to inconsistent walking speed, not the product. Calibrate whenever you switch products — granule size changes flow rate dramatically.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how much fertilizer to use?

Grams of product = (nitrogen needed per m² × area) ÷ (N% ÷ 100). For 5 g N/m² over 10 m² with 20% N fertilizer: (5×10) ÷ 0.20 = 250 g.

What do the three numbers on fertilizer mean?

20-10-10 means 20% nitrogen, 10% phosphate (P₂O₅) and 10% potash (K₂O) by weight. The rest is carrier material.

How do I know if my plants need nitrogen?

Nitrogen shows as uniform yellowing of older lower leaves plus stunted growth. Confirm with a soil test before treating.

Can too much fertilizer burn plants?

Yes — excess fertilizer salts pull water from roots and scorch foliage. Never exceed label rates, water granules in immediately, and halve doses for seedlings.

Should I fertilize in winter?

Stop feeding most houseplants in winter when growth slows; resume in spring. Lawns and outdoor beds also rest — feed autumn lawns once, then nothing until spring.

Is organic fertilizer better than synthetic?

Synthetics give exact, fast nutrition for specific deficiencies; organics feed soil biology and rarely burn. Many gardeners use compost as the base plus targeted synthetics.
