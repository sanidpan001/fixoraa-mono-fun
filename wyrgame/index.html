What is Would You Rather?

Would You Rather is one of the oldest parlour games in the world because its rules fit in a single sentence: you are offered two options, and you must pick one. No skipping, no “neither”, no inventing a third choice. The magic is in the dilemma — each option has to be appealing enough to consider and painful enough to make the choice genuinely hard. Played well, a single would you rather question can fuel twenty minutes of argument, laughter, and surprising revelations about how your friends think.

How to play

The classic format needs nothing but people. One player reads a question aloud, everyone commits to an answer, and then the debate begins: each side argues why their choice is better, funnier, or merely less terrible. Common scoring twists keep it lively:

  • Majority rules: the side with more votes wins the round.
  • Versus mode: two players go head to head, and the group judges whose pick was braver.
  • Devil's advocate: after choosing, you must argue for the option you rejected.
  • Lightning round: ten seconds per question, no overthinking — gut answers only.

What makes a great dilemma

Not every pair of options works. The best questions share a hidden structure worth knowing if you ever write your own:

Balanced trade-offs

“Would you rather be rich or poor?” is a dead question — everyone picks rich. Great dilemmas pit two genuine goods against each other (“unlimited money but no friends” vs “deep friendships but never comfortable”) or two genuine bads (“hiccups forever” vs “sneeze every time you laugh”). The closer to 50/50 the room splits, the better the question.

Concrete consequences

Abstract questions (“truth or happiness?”) sound deep but play flat, because nobody knows what they are really choosing. Concrete questions (“know the date of your death” vs “know the cause”) force you to imagine living with the answer — and that imagination is where the fun lives.

A personal hook

The question should reveal something about the answerer. Food dilemmas reveal priorities, adventure dilemmas reveal appetite for risk, deep dilemmas reveal values. That is why the game doubles as an icebreaker: ten questions in, you know people better than after an hour of small talk.

The four categories

Funny

Silly, low-stakes dilemmas perfect for warming up a group or playing with kids. Nobody's values are on trial; the only goal is the laugh when someone defends wearing socks on their hands with total sincerity.

Deep

Questions about mortality, memory, love, and meaning. Save these for when the group is comfortable — they can turn a party game into a genuinely moving conversation. Also excellent writing prompts.

Food

Deceptively divisive. People hold shockingly strong opinions about pizza, condiments, and breakfast foods. Food rounds work brilliantly at dinner parties, where the props are already on the table.

Adventure

Travel, exploration, and bucket-list choices. Great for trip planning (“space or ocean?” settles more than you'd think) and for daydreaming with friends.

The psychology of the dilemma

Psychologists love forced-choice questions because they expose decision-making styles. Some people optimise — they calculate which option has the better expected outcome. Others satisfice — they pick the first option that feels livable and defend it. Some choose to minimise regret (“which would I regret less at 80?”), others to maximise story value (“which makes the better anecdote?”). Watching a group reveals these styles fast, which is why the game never gets old: the questions are fixed, but the reasoning is endlessly human.

Writing your own questions

Once the built-in bank runs out, make your own with this formula: take something the group loves, and threaten it. “Would you rather give up your phone or give up weekends?” works because both hurt. Personalise ruthlessly — a question about the group's shared experiences (“our road trip playlist or our group chat?”) will always beat a generic one. Test each question on yourself first: if your answer is instant and obvious, sharpen one side until choosing hurts a little.

House rules worth stealing

  • No “neither”. The entire game collapses the moment opting out is allowed. Enforce the pick.
  • Defend your choice. A vote without a reason doesn't score.
  • One veto per night. Anyone can skip one question that genuinely upsets them — no questions asked.
  • Winner picks next. The round winner chooses the next category, keeping everyone invested.
  • Keep it kind. Deep questions can touch real nerves; read the room and move on cheerfully if one lands wrong.

Would You Rather as a writing prompt

Writers have quietly used forced dilemmas for decades. Pick any deep question and you have a story seed: a character who knows the date of their death, a protagonist offered unlimited money at the cost of every friendship. The dilemma is a plot engine — it forces a choice, and choices reveal character, which is all fiction really is. Try this exercise: take a question, write 300 words from each side, and see which version surprises you. Teachers use the same trick for persuasive essays: “defend the option you didn't pick” produces stronger arguments than any assigned topic, because the student has genuine skin in the game.

Ten famously hard dilemmas to try

  1. Know how the world ends, but be unable to stop it — or live in blissful ignorance?
  2. Be able to fly, but only at walking speed — or be invisible, but only when nobody is looking?
  3. Have every song you hear be slightly out of tune — or every conversation slightly out of sync?
  4. Live 10 years as a billionaire — or 100 years comfortably?
  5. Always know when someone is lying — or always get away with lying yourself?
  6. Give up your favourite food forever — or give up music forever?
  7. Be the funniest person in every room — or the wisest?
  8. Relive the same perfect year forever — or keep ageing with all its uncertainty?
  9. Have unlimited free time but no money — or unlimited money but no free time?
  10. Know everyone's thoughts for a day — or have no one know yours ever again?

Notice what makes these sting: each one trades something you love against something else you love. That is the anatomy of a great dilemma, and now you can spot it — which means you can build your own.

Playing with kids

Would You Rather is a superb game for children because there are no wrong answers and no way to lose. Stick to the Funny and Food categories, keep the dilemmas concrete (kids choose better when they can picture both options), and let them invent the follow-up: “what would you do on day three of hiccups forever?” Their answers are reliably more creative than adults'. The game also builds decision-making muscles — committing to a choice and defending it is genuinely good cognitive practice disguised as silliness.

The art of the follow-up question

The dilemma is only the opening move — the real game is the follow-up. “Why?” is the simplest and best: it forces reasons instead of vibes. Then escalate: “What would change your mind?” reveals how strongly someone holds their pick. “What would your ten-year-old self choose?” adds nostalgia and often flips the answer entirely. The legendary follow-up is the modifier round: take the winning option and add a cost (“okay, but now it costs you your weekends”) until the room flips. Played this way, a single good dilemma can sustain an entire evening — the question starts the fire, but the follow-ups keep it burning.
