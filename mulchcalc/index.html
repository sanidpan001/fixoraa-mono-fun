How much mulch do you need? The formula

Mulch volume is simple geometry: cubic yards = (area in sq ft × depth in inches) ÷ 324. The 324 comes from converting units — 1 cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, and depth in inches divided by 12 gives feet, so area × (depth/12) ÷ 27 = area × depth ÷ 324. A 200 sq ft bed at 3 inches deep needs 200 × 3 ÷ 324 = 1.85 cubic yards. Most bagged mulch comes in 2 cubic-foot bags, and one cubic yard equals 13.5 such bags — so 1.85 yards is 25 bags.

How deep should mulch be?

SituationDepthWhy
New beds, bare soil3–4 inchesSuppresses weeds, retains moisture
Refreshing existing mulch1–2 inchesTop-up only — don't bury the old layer deeper
Around trees2–3 inchesKeep 3–6 inches clear of the trunk
Vegetable gardens2–3 inchesStraw or shredded leaves preferred

More is not better: mulch deeper than 4 inches can suffocate roots, trap excess moisture against stems, and invite rodents. The infamous "mulch volcano" piled against tree trunks causes bark rot and girdling roots — always leave a doughnut-shaped gap around trunks.

Bulk vs bagged mulch: the price maths

Bagged mulch typically costs $3–5 per 2 cu ft bag. Bulk mulch from a landscape yard runs $25–45 per cubic yard — equivalent to roughly $2–3.30 per bag-equivalent. The crossover: for under ~1.5 cubic yards (20 bags), bagged is usually cheaper once delivery fees are counted; above that, bulk wins decisively. A 500 sq ft bed at 3 inches needs 4.6 yards — about $150–200 bulk versus $250+ bagged. Bulk delivery often has a minimum (commonly 2–3 yards) and a delivery fee, so factor that in.

Types of mulch and where to use them

  • Shredded bark: the all-rounder — knits together, resists washing away, good for slopes.
  • Wood chips: cheap and long-lasting; best for paths and around trees, not against stems (nitrogen tie-up at the soil surface).
  • Straw: ideal for vegetable gardens — light, easy to move, breaks down fast.
  • Compost: feeds soil as it mulches; use 1–2 inches as a top-dress on beds.
  • Stone/gravel: permanent, zero maintenance, but heats soil and never improves it — reserve for cactus beds and drainage areas.
  • Dyed mulch: looks tidy but the colour fades in a season; undyed bark ages to a natural brown anyway.

How to spread mulch properly

Weed first — mulch suppresses new weeds but will not kill established ones. Edge the bed for a crisp line that keeps mulch in and grass out. Spread evenly with a rake, keeping mulch 3–6 inches from stems and trunks. Water after spreading to settle it. In spring, fluff matted winter mulch with a rake before topping up so water penetrates instead of sheeting off.

When and how often to mulch

Mulch in late spring after soil warms — mulching too early keeps soil cold and slows growth. Top up once a year; organic mulches decompose 1–2 inches annually, so a 1–2 inch refresh maintains depth without overbuilding. In autumn, shredded fallen leaves make free mulch — run the mower over them and spread 2–3 inches on beds.

Common mistakes

  • Volcano mulching trees: bark rot, pests, girdling roots. Keep the trunk flare visible.
  • Too deep: over 4 inches suffocates roots and shelters slugs.
  • Mulching weeds: pull or smother first; mulch is prevention, not cure.
  • Fresh wood chips against stems: fine on paths, but keep fresh chips away from plant bases.

Measure the bed, pick the depth, run the numbers — and buy once, not twice.

Mulch and soil pH: the real story

Pine needles and bark are often blamed for acidifying soil, but research shows the effect is negligible — mulch breaks down slowly and soil buffers pH strongly. Fresh wood chips can temporarily tie up nitrogen at the soil surface as microbes decompose them, which is why you keep fresh chips away from plant stems but can use them freely on paths. If a soil test shows acidity, lime corrects it far more effectively than changing mulch type. Choose mulch for texture, cost and appearance; manage pH separately.

Living mulches and groundcovers

The cheapest mulch is a plant. Low groundcovers — creeping thyme, sedum, clover, sweet woodruff — suppress weeds, hold moisture and never need topping up. White clover even fixes nitrogen, feeding neighbouring plants. Living mulch suits areas where you will not walk much and do not need a crisp formal edge. Many gardeners combine approaches: bark mulch for the first year while groundcover plugs establish, then let the plants take over.

Measuring irregular beds

Real gardens are not rectangles. For curved beds, break the shape into rough rectangles and triangles, estimate each area, and add them — precision to the nearest 10 sq ft is plenty since you will round up bags anyway. For a quick field estimate, pace the bed: one adult pace is roughly 2.5 ft, so length in paces × width in paces × 6.25 gives square feet. Always round up to whole bags or half yards; a little extra mulch finds a use somewhere.

Sheet mulching: killing grass without digging

Converting lawn to garden beds? Sheet mulching (lasagna gardening) does it with zero digging: mow the grass short, lay overlapping cardboard (tape removed), wet it thoroughly, then pile 4–6 inches of compost and mulch on top. In 2–3 months the grass underneath dies and decomposes, and you plant straight through. A 100 sq ft conversion needs about 2 cubic yards of material — run it through the calculator with 5-inch depth to estimate. It is slower than a sod cutter but far less work and builds soil instead of removing it.

Rubber mulch: an honest assessment

Shredded-tyre mulch lasts essentially forever and does not attract termites, which sells a lot of playgrounds. But it does not feed soil, it heats up dramatically in sun (surface temperatures 20–30°F above wood mulch), it can leach zinc and other tyre compounds, and stray pieces migrate everywhere. For play areas where permanence matters, it has a case. For garden beds, it is all downside: plants want organic matter, not rubber. Wood mulch costs less per year once you account for rubber's premium price.

Mulch rings around trees: the right way

A proper mulch ring extends to the tree's drip line where feasible, 2–3 inches deep, with a clear 3–6 inch gap at the trunk so the root flare stays visible and dry. This single practice does more for urban tree health than almost anything else: it eliminates mower and string-trimmer damage (the #1 killer of young street trees), retains soil moisture, and moderates root-zone temperature. For a tree with a 6 ft diameter ring at 3 inches, you need about 0.65 cubic yards — roughly 9 bags. Refresh yearly without deepening.

Dyed vs natural mulch colour

Black and red dyed mulches use iron oxide and carbon-based colorants generally regarded as safe, and they hold colour one season before fading to brown-grey. Natural shredded bark starts brown and silvers gracefully. The practical difference is mostly aesthetic and short-lived — within a year, dyed and undyed look similar. Dyed mulch sometimes contains recycled wood of uncertain origin, so for vegetable gardens, stick to natural bark, straw or compost. Buy colour for instant curb appeal; buy natural for everything else.

A worked example: mulching a whole front yard

Say your front yard has three beds: 120 sq ft, 80 sq ft and a 60 sq ft tree ring, all needing 3 inches. Total area = 260 sq ft; volume = 260 × 3 ÷ 324 ≈ 2.4 cubic yards, or about 33 bags (2.4 × 13.5). Bulk at $35/yard = $84 plus a $50 delivery fee = $134; bagged at $4/bag = $132. At this size it is nearly a tie — but add one more bed and bulk wins clearly. This is exactly the crossover the calculator flags: price both options before you buy.

Ordering from the yard: what to ask

When you call a landscape supplier, ask four things: the minimum delivery (usually 2–3 yards), the delivery fee and truck access (they need a clear dumping spot), whether the mulch is aged or fresh (aged is better near plants), and the pickup price per yard if you have a truck — pickup often skips the fee entirely. Order 10% more than the calculator says; settling and uneven spreading eat the margin.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how much mulch I need?

Cubic yards = (area in sq ft × depth in inches) ÷ 324. A 200 sq ft bed at 3 inches needs 1.85 cubic yards, or about 25 two-cubic-foot bags.

How deep should mulch be?

3 inches for new beds, 1–2 inches to refresh existing mulch, 2–3 inches around trees (kept clear of trunks). Never exceed 4 inches.

How many bags of mulch are in a cubic yard?

One cubic yard equals 13.5 two-cubic-foot bags. Multiply your cubic yards by 13.5 and round up.

Is bulk or bagged mulch cheaper?

For under ~1.5 cubic yards, bagged is usually cheaper after delivery fees. Above that, bulk mulch ($25–45/yard) wins decisively.

Why is volcano mulching bad for trees?

It causes bark rot, invites pests and creates girdling roots. Keep mulch 3–6 inches from trunks with the root flare visible.

When is the best time to mulch?

Late spring, after soil has warmed — mulching too early keeps soil cold. Top up once a year; organic mulches decompose 1–2 inches annually.
