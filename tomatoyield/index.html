How many tomatoes will your plants produce?

A healthy tomato plant is one of the most productive plants you can grow, but yields vary enormously by variety, which is why a yield estimate has to start with the type you are growing. Cherry tomatoes produce many small fruits, typically 4 kilograms per plant over a season in good conditions. Salad or round varieties produce around 6 kilograms per plant, plum tomatoes about 5 kilograms, and large beefsteak varieties can reach 8 kilograms per plant when they are well fed and well supported. Multiply plants by per-plant yield and you have your season total: 10 salad plants at 6 kilograms each give about 60 kilograms, which is a serious amount of tomatoes, enough for daily salads, cooking, and preserving.

Those figures assume decent conditions: full sun, consistent watering, regular feeding, and plants that are pruned and supported. A plant in shade, starved of water, or left to sprawl on the ground will produce far less, and disease can cut a crop short by weeks. Conversely, a greenhouse plant with a long season, regular feeding, and careful pruning can beat these averages, especially indeterminate varieties that keep growing and fruiting until frost. Outdoor plants in cooler climates have a shorter season, so expect the lower end of the range, and plan for a glut in late summer when most of the crop ripens at once.

Variety guide: what to expect

Cherry tomatoes are the most reliable croppers for beginners, because they fruit early, ripen quickly, and keep producing even when conditions are imperfect. Their small size means the weight per plant looks modest, but the number of fruits is huge, and they are perfect for snacking and salads. Salad varieties, the classic round red tomatoes, are the all-rounders, good for slicing, cooking, and salads, with a solid mid-range yield. Beefsteak tomatoes produce fewer but much larger fruits, ideal for slicing and sandwiches, though they take longer to ripen and need strong support. Plum tomatoes have dense, low-seed flesh that cooks down beautifully into sauces and passata, making them the preserving gardener's favourite.

How many plants does a household need?

Most households overplant tomatoes, because a single plant's yield is hard to picture until the glut arrives. Two to four plants are plenty for a couple who eats salads a few times a week, giving 12 to 24 kilograms over the season. A family that cooks with tomatoes regularly will use six to ten plants happily, and anyone who wants to make sauce, soup, and passata for winter should plant ten to twenty, with at least half being plum varieties. Remember that the crop does not arrive evenly: you will get a trickle in early summer, a flood in late summer, and a final rush before the first frost, so plan preserving, freezing, and giving away accordingly.

Spacing, support, and pruning

Tomato yield depends heavily on support and pruning. Indeterminate or cordon varieties grow tall and must be tied to canes, strings, or frames, with side shoots pinched out regularly so the plant puts energy into fruit rather than foliage. Determinate or bush varieties stay compact and need less pruning, but they still benefit from a cage or stake to keep fruit off the ground. Space plants 45 to 60 centimetres apart in rows 75 centimetres apart, so air circulates and light reaches the lower leaves. Crowded plants look lush but fruit poorly, since shaded leaves cannot photosynthesise well and damp, still air encourages blight.

Watering and feeding for maximum yield

Consistent watering is the single biggest factor in tomato yield and quality after sunlight. Irregular watering causes split fruit and blossom end rot, a dark patch on the fruit base caused by calcium uptake problems when watering fluctuates. Water deeply at the base, not from overhead, and keep the soil evenly moist, mulching to reduce evaporation. Feed weekly once the first truss has set, using a high-potash tomato feed, and do not overfeed with nitrogen, which produces lush leaves at the expense of fruit. Container plants need more frequent watering and feeding than plants in the ground, since their root zone is small and dries out fast.

Greenhouse versus outdoor growing

Greenhouse tomatoes usually out-yield outdoor plants, because they get a longer season, warmer nights, and protection from rain-borne blight. A greenhouse plant can start earlier, fruit sooner, and keep going until late autumn, which adds weeks of cropping. Outdoor plants, however, often taste better, since they get more direct sun and natural temperature swings, and they need no ventilation management. In cooler climates, choose early, blight-resistant outdoor varieties and plant in the sunniest, most sheltered spot available, against a south-facing wall if possible. In warm climates, outdoor growing is easy, and the challenge is watering and pest control rather than warmth.

Dealing with the glut

Every tomato grower eventually faces the glut, when ripe tomatoes arrive faster than they can be eaten. The best strategy is to have a plan before it happens. Eat the best fresh, cook the next tier into sauce, soup, and ratatouille, and freeze the rest: whole cherry tomatoes freeze beautifully for cooking, and larger tomatoes can be frozen whole, then skinned under running water when needed. Passata and chutney are excellent for large quantities, and they store for months. Giving away is also a legitimate strategy, since neighbours rarely refuse a bag of homegrown tomatoes, and it builds goodwill for next year's seed swaps.

Pests, diseases, and protecting your yield

Blight is the disease every tomato grower fears, since it can destroy a crop in days in warm, wet weather. Choose resistant varieties, space plants for airflow, water at the base, and remove affected leaves immediately. Whitefly and aphids are common in greenhouses, and they can be managed with sticky traps, companion planting, and biological controls. Slugs attack low fruit on outdoor plants, so keep fruit off the ground with supports and mulch. A small amount of loss is normal and should be built into your expectations, which is another reason to plant one or two extra plants beyond your calculated need.

Succession and season extension

You can stretch the tomato season at both ends with a little planning. Start seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last frost, harden plants off gradually, and plant out only when nights are reliably above 10 degrees. At the end of the season, when frost threatens, pick green tomatoes and ripen them indoors in a paper bag with a banana, which releases ethylene that speeds ripening. Alternatively, make green tomato chutney, a delicious use for fruit that would otherwise be lost. A second, later sowing of cherry tomatoes can also extend the season, since they ripen faster than large varieties.

Container growing: yields in pots

Container tomatoes can yield nearly as well as ground-grown plants if the pot is large enough, at least 30 centimetres across and deep, with good compost and regular feeding. Small pots restrict roots, dry out quickly, and cap yields, so go bigger than you think you need. Bush varieties suit containers best, since they need less support, but cordon varieties work too if you provide a sturdy cane. Water containers daily in hot weather, sometimes twice, and feed weekly, since nutrients wash out of pots faster than from garden soil.

Seed saving and next year's crop

Saving seed from your best plants is free, satisfying, and lets you select for your own garden over time. Choose open-pollinated varieties, not hybrids, since hybrid seed does not come true. Ferment the seed in its own juice for a few days, rinse, dry thoroughly, and store in a cool, dry place. Label everything with variety and year, since tomato seeds all look alike and you will not remember which envelope is which by spring. Saved seed stays viable for several years, so a good year can supply your garden and your neighbours for seasons to come.

Frequently asked questions

How many kilograms of tomatoes does one plant produce?

About 4 kg for cherry, 5 kg for plum, 6 kg for salad varieties, and up to 8 kg for beefsteak, in good conditions with sun, water, and feeding.

How many tomato plants do I need for a family?

Six to ten plants suit a family that cooks with tomatoes regularly. Two to four suit a couple eating salads a few times a week.

Why are my tomatoes splitting?

Irregular watering is the usual cause. Water deeply and consistently at the base, and mulch to keep soil moisture even.

Do greenhouse tomatoes yield more?

Usually yes, because of a longer season, warmer nights, and protection from rain-borne blight, though outdoor tomatoes often taste better.

What is blossom end rot?

A dark patch on the fruit base caused by calcium uptake problems when watering fluctuates. Consistent watering prevents it.

Should I prune tomato plants?

Cordon varieties yes: pinch out side shoots regularly so energy goes into fruit. Bush varieties need little pruning beyond support.

How do I ripen green tomatoes at the end of the season?

Pick them before frost and ripen indoors in a paper bag with a banana, which releases ethylene that speeds ripening.

Can I grow tomatoes in pots?

Yes, in pots at least 30 cm across with good compost, daily watering in hot weather, and weekly feeding. Bush varieties suit pots best.
