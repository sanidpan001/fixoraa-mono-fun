About ChiliHeat — scoville heat mixer calculator

The Scoville scale measures heat by the concentration of capsaicinoids, the compounds that make peppers burn, and it is still the language cooks use to compare peppers. A jalapeño sits roughly between 2,500 and 8,000 Scoville Heat Units, a cayenne between 30,000 and 50,000, and a habanero between 100,000 and 350,000, while a bell pepper scores zero because it contains no capsaicin. Those ranges are wide because heat varies with variety, growing conditions, ripeness and even position on the plant. ChiliHeat does not pretend a single number describes every pepper: it takes the ratings you enter, which may come from a seed packet, a supplier or a recipe, and blends them honestly by weight, so the result reflects your actual ingredients rather than a generic label.

Blending is a weighted average, not a simple average of the two ratings. If you mix 100 grams of a 5,000 SHU pepper with 50 grams of a 30,000 SHU pepper, the result is not 17,500 SHU, the midpoint of the two ratings. The mild pepper dominates because there is twice as much of it, so the calculation is 500,000 plus 1,500,000, divided by 150 grams, giving about 13,333 SHU. That distinction matters in practice: a cook who averages ratings instead of weighting them will overshoot heat badly when a small amount of very hot pepper is involved, or undershoot when a large mild base dilutes a hot addition. Weight, not volume, is the right basis, because peppers vary in density and a cup of chopped habanero weighs far more than a cup of sliced jalapeño.

Perceived heat is not the same as measured heat, and a good sauce maker plans for both. Fat and dairy soften the burn because capsaicin dissolves in fat, so a creamy sauce tastes milder than a vinegar sauce at the same SHU. Sugar and sweetness also mask heat, while acid brightens it and can make the same sauce feel sharper. Salt, temperature and alcohol change perception too, and individual tolerance varies enormously. That is why ChiliHeat labels its result with a broad level, mild, medium, hot or very hot, and advises tasting a tiny amount first. Use the calculator to get into the right neighbourhood, then adjust with fat, acid or sweetness, and retest, rather than chasing an exact number that no tongue can verify.

Choosing peppers is about flavour as much as fire, and the best blends pair a flavour base with a heat lift. Jalapeños bring a fresh, grassy note and moderate heat, poblanos add earthy depth at low heat, and cayenne adds clean, straightforward fire without much flavour of its own. Habaneros contribute a fruity, almost tropical aroma that suits Caribbean and fruit-based sauces, while Scotch bonnets offer a similar fruitiness with a slightly different character. Chipotles, which are smoked dried jalapeños, add smoke and depth. A thoughtful blend might use a large mild base for body and flavour, a medium pepper for warmth, and a small amount of a very hot pepper for lift, and ChiliHeat lets you test that idea in two-pepper steps before you chop anything.

Safety deserves respect when handling hot peppers, especially above 50,000 SHU. Wear gloves, avoid touching your face and eyes, wash hands and equipment thoroughly, and work in a ventilated area when cooking large batches, because airborne capsaicin from frying or blending can irritate lungs and eyes. Taste with a clean spoon, in tiny amounts, and wait a minute before judging, because heat builds rather than arriving instantly. Keep hot sauces labelled with their ingredients and an honest heat warning, particularly if children or guests with low tolerance will eat them. If someone is overwhelmed by heat, offer milk, yoghurt or bread rather than water, which spreads capsaicin rather than dissolving it.

Scaling a recipe up or down is where a calculator earns its keep. Doubling a sauce does not change its SHU if the proportions stay the same, but changing the ratio of hot to mild peppers does, and small errors scale with the batch. If a test batch of 150 grams tastes right, write down the exact weights and ratings, not just the pepper names, because next season's peppers will differ. For a large commercial-style batch, make a small test blend first, confirm the heat, then scale the weights proportionally. ChiliHeat's two-pepper model can be reused in stages for three or more peppers: blend the first two, note the result and total weight, then blend that result with the third pepper using the same weighted-average logic.

Common mistakes are easy to spot once you understand weighting. Averaging ratings without weights is the first, and it always overstates the effect of the smaller ingredient. Using volume instead of weight is the second, because a loosely packed cup and a tightly packed cup are different amounts. Ignoring seeds and membranes is the third: much of a pepper's heat lives in the white pith and seeds, so removing them lowers heat and keeping them raises it, even at the same pepper weight. Finally, tasting straight from a hot pan overstates heat, because temperature amplifies the burn, so let a sauce cool to serving temperature before making a final judgement about its level.

Worked examples anchor the method. Mixing 100 grams at 5,000 SHU with 50 grams at 30,000 SHU gives 13,333 SHU, a solidly hot result. Mixing equal weights at 10,000 SHU gives 10,000 SHU, as expected, because neither pepper pulls the average. Mixing 10 grams at 50,000 SHU with 90 grams at 2,000 SHU gives 6,800 SHU, showing how a small hot addition lifts a mild base without overwhelming it. Try your own peppers, keep notes on flavour as well as heat, and you will build a personal sense of which blends suit salsa, curry, hot sauce and chilli con carne.

The Scoville scale itself has an interesting history that explains why its numbers feel both precise and approximate at the same time. Wilbur Scoville invented it in 1912 by diluting pepper extracts until a panel of tasters could no longer detect heat, so the original unit was a dilution factor, not a chemical measurement. Modern laboratories measure capsaicinoids directly with chromatography and convert the result to Scoville units, which is more consistent, but the underlying variation remains, because two peppers of the same variety grown in different soils, climates and seasons can differ by thousands of units. That variation is why recipes that name only a pepper, without a weight or a heat note, are unreliable, and why a calculator that uses your actual peppers and weights is more honest than a table of generic values. Capsaicin itself is a remarkable compound: it triggers the same receptors as heat, which is why the brain reports burning, even though no tissue is actually being burned, and it is used in pain-relief creams for exactly that reason. Tolerance also builds with exposure, so a regular chilli eater and a first-time taster will judge the same sauce very differently, and a good host labels heat clearly and offers a mild alternative. When developing a sauce for others, aim for the middle of your audience's tolerance, not your own, and remember that heat can be added at the table with a hotter sauce, but it cannot easily be removed from a pot that is already too hot. Freeze small test batches with labels, compare them side by side after a week, and you will learn how heat changes with time, because capsaicin mellows slightly in storage while flavours meld and deepen.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate blended Scoville heat?

Use a weighted average: multiply each pepper's SHU by its weight, add the results, and divide by the total weight. ChiliHeat does this for two peppers.

Why is a weighted average better than a simple average?

Because the pepper you use more of should influence the result more. A simple average overstates a small amount of very hot pepper.

What SHU is considered hot?

Roughly: under 1,000 is mild, 1,000–10,000 is medium, 10,000–50,000 is hot, and above 50,000 is very hot — but individual tolerance varies a lot.

Does removing seeds reduce heat?

Yes. Much of a pepper's heat is in the white pith and seeds, so removing them lowers the perceived heat at the same pepper weight.

Why does the same sauce taste hotter when hot?

Temperature amplifies the burn, and fat, sugar and acid also change perception. Taste sauces at serving temperature for a fair judgement.

How do I calm the burn if a sauce is too hot?

Add fat, dairy, sugar or more mild base ingredients. Offer milk or yoghurt to eaters, not water, which spreads capsaicin.
