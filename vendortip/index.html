Who Actually Gets a Tip?

Wedding tipping has no single rulebook, which is exactly why couples panic about it in the final week. The working principle: tip the people who perform a personal service on the day, not necessarily the business owners behind them. Your photographer, DJ, hair and makeup team, delivery drivers, and the catering captain all did hands-on work for you; the stationery designer who mailed your invitations three months ago did not. When in doubt, generosity toward day-of workers is never wrong — these are long, physical days.

The checklist above covers the twelve vendors couples most often wonder about, each with the going norm. But norms are starting points. A DJ who read the room perfectly and kept your dance floor packed until midnight earned more than the baseline; a florist who simply dropped off centerpieces and left earned the baseline. Tip for the service you received, not the invoice you paid.

The Service-Charge Trap

Before tipping anyone, read your contracts for the words "service charge." This is the most expensive misunderstanding in wedding tipping. A service charge — often 18 to 24 percent on catering and venue contracts — is a mandatory fee that goes to the house, not a gratuity for the staff. Many couples see it, assume the staff is covered, and tip nothing (leaving workers short); others pay the service charge and a full 20 percent tip on top (double-paying by thousands).

The fix is one email per vendor: "Does the service charge go to the service staff as gratuity, or is it a house fee?" If it goes to staff, you are done — check "in contract" in the calculator and move on. If it is a house fee, tip the staff separately on the pre-tax subtotal. Note "pre-tax": tipping on the total including service charge and tax quietly inflates the tip by 25 percent or more.

Vendor by Vendor: The Norms

Wedding planner / day-of coordinator: $200–$500 for full-service planning, $100–$200 for day-of coordination. If they own the company, tipping is optional — a detailed review and referrals carry real value. Photographer: $100–$200 (more for a second shooter who hustled). Videographer: $100–$200. DJ or band: $100–$150 for a DJ, $25–$50 per band member. Florist: $50–$100, mainly for setup and delivery crews rather than design time.

Catering staff: 15–20% of the food and beverage subtotal, given to the catering captain to distribute — or confirm the house distributes a service charge as gratuity. Bartenders: 10% of the bar bill, or $1–$2 per drink if the bar is hosted. Cake delivery: $25–$50, more for complex on-site assembly. Officiant: $100–$200 for a professional; a donation to the house of worship for clergy. Hair and makeup: 20% of the beauty bill, like a salon visit. Transportation: 15% of the bill if not already included. Venue coordinator: $100–$250 if they went beyond the venue's basic duties.

The Envelope System

Do not plan to "figure it out on the day." A week before the wedding, withdraw the total in cash (small bills help — five $20s beat one $100 when you need to adjust), stuff labeled envelopes ("DJ — Marcus — $125"), and hand the stack to one trusted person: your planner, maid of honor, or best man. Write a tiny cheat sheet of when each goes out: delivery vendors on arrival, ceremony musicians after the ceremony, photographer and DJ at night's end.

Two details people forget: checks work too — several vendors prefer them, and a check in the envelope with a thank-you note feels intentional rather than transactional. And build in a 10 percent flex buffer above the calculator's total; someone will exceed expectations, and having the cash ready lets you reward it in the moment instead of mailing regrets later.

Budgeting: The 5–10% Rule

Most couples land between 5 and 10 percent of the total wedding budget on tips. On a $30,000 wedding that is $1,500–$3,000 — real money that should be a line item from the first budget draft, not a discovery the week of. The percentage skews higher on smaller weddings (fixed per-vendor tips dominate) and lower on very large ones (catering percentages scale, but many vendors stay flat).

If the total stings, remember what it buys: the goodwill of every person executing the most photographed day of your life. Well-tipped vendors stay later, fix problems faster, and remember you fondly when you need anniversary-session bookings. It is the highest-ROI line in the entire budget.

Regional and Cultural Notes

Norms shift by region — major metros run 10–20% above these baselines, and destination weddings often follow local customs rather than yours (ask your planner). In some cultures, tipping individual vendors is uncommon and a single generous gift to the coordinator is the norm; in others, cash in red envelopes carries specific meaning. There is no universal etiquette, only thoughtfulness: when uncertain, ask your planner what is customary here, and err generous.

What If the Budget Is Tight?

Tips are customary, not compulsory — no vendor will chase you for one. If money is tight, prioritize in this order: catering captain and bartenders (hourly workers who depend on gratuities), hair and makeup (salon norms apply), then delivery/setup crews. For the rest, a sincere handwritten note plus a five-star review posted within a week is genuinely valuable — vendors consistently say reviews drive more business than any single tip. What you cannot do is promise tips to negotiate a lower fee and then vanish; that burns a bridge in an industry with a long memory.

Tipping at Destination Weddings

Destination weddings rewrite the tipping script. First, local custom beats home custom — in many Caribbean and Mexican resorts, a service charge is built into the package and genuinely reaches staff, while in parts of Europe and Asia, tipping individual vendors is uncommon and a single gift to the coordinator is the norm. Your planner or venue coordinator is the authority here: ask explicitly, "What do couples typically do for vendor gratuities here?" before you import your home country's habits.

Second, logistics get harder. You cannot easily stuff cash envelopes in a foreign currency you have never handled — order local currency before you fly, in small denominations, and keep a written conversion cheat sheet (a $100 tip is not $100 in euros or pesos). Some resorts forbid cash tipping to staff entirely and route everything through the front desk; others expect it. Third, vendors you flew in (photographer, planner) get tipped on the same norms as at home, plus consideration for the travel — they gave up a weekend and crossed an ocean for you. A 20% bump on their usual tip, or a thoughtful experience gift, lands well.

The Thank-You Note Multiplier

Here is the industry secret: vendors consistently say a specific, prompt, public review is worth more than a $50 larger tip. Not "great vendor, highly recommend" — specific: "Marcus read a dead dance floor at 9 p.m. and had three generations doing the cha-cha slide by 9:20." That sentence books them their next three weddings. Pair every tip envelope with a handwritten note naming what they did brilliantly, then post the review within a week while the details are vivid.

Referrals are the second currency. Tag vendors in your wedding photos on social media (with permission), send them your favorite professional shots for their portfolio, and recommend them by name when engaged friends ask. Photographers and planners run on this oxygen. The couples vendors remember — and prioritize for anniversary shoots, vow renewals, and "can you squeeze us in" favors — are the ones who tipped fairly and told the world. Cash ends the transaction; gratitude extends the relationship.

Frequently asked questions

How much should you tip wedding vendors?

Typical norms: $100–$200 each for photographer and videographer, $100–$150 for the DJ, 15–20% of the catering bill for service staff, 20% of hair and makeup, 15% for transportation, $50–$150 for delivery crews. Check contracts first — a built-in service charge means no extra tip is expected.

Do you tip the wedding photographer?

It is customary but not mandatory — $100 to $200 is usual, more for exceptional service. If the photographer owns the studio, a heartfelt review and referrals matter as much as cash.

What is the difference between a service charge and a gratuity?

A service charge is a mandatory house fee that may not reach staff at all. A gratuity is voluntary and goes to the workers. Ask each vendor in writing whether their service charge is distributed as gratuity before you tip on top.

When do you give vendors their tips?

Prepare labeled cash envelopes a week ahead and delegate distribution to your planner or maid of honor. Delivery vendors get theirs on arrival; photographer, DJ and coordinator at the end of the night.

Do you tip the wedding planner?

$200–$500 for full-service planning, $100–$200 for day-of coordination. If the planner owns the business, tipping is optional — reviews and referrals are equally valued.

How much of the wedding budget goes to tips?

Most couples spend 5–10% of the total budget on tips. Make it a line item from the first budget draft so it never becomes a last-week surprise.
