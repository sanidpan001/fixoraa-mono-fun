What Dehumidifier Size Actually Means

Dehumidifier capacity is measured in pints per day — the volume of water the unit pulls from the air in 24 hours. A 30-pint unit removes 30 pints daily; a 70-pint unit removes 70. The number on the box is tested at AHAM standard conditions (80°F and 60% relative humidity), which matters more than most shoppers realize: a unit rated 70 pints in a warm test lab may deliver only 50 to 55 effective pints in a cool 65°F basement, because cold air holds less moisture and the compressor works harder. If your space runs cool, size up — this tool's calculator applies that correction automatically.

Size is the single decision that determines whether you enjoy dry air in three months or are still fighting humidity a year later. An undersized unit runs constantly, never quite reaches the target humidity, and burns out early. An oversized unit short-cycles — clicking on and off — which wastes energy and shortens compressor life. The goal is the smallest unit that handles your peak load with about 25 to 30 percent of headroom, not the biggest box you can afford.

The Two Variables That Decide Everything

Every credible sizing method starts with the same two inputs: square footage and dampness level. Measure the space (length × width; estimate a little generously for odd shapes), then classify the dampness honestly:

  • Moderately damp (50–60% RH): slight mustiness in humid weather, minor condensation on cold surfaces.
  • Very damp (60–70% RH): persistent musty smell, visible condensation, the room always feels wet.
  • Wet (70–80% RH): damp spots on walls or floor, sweating pipes, possible mold growth.
  • Extremely wet: standing water, seepage, or severe mold — fix the water source first, then dehumidify.

The working rule this tool uses: a 500 sq ft space needs about 10 pints/day moderately damp, 12 very damp, 14 wet, and 16 extremely wet — plus roughly 4 to 5 pints for each additional 500 sq ft. A 1,000 sq ft very-damp basement therefore lands around 24 pints before corrections. When in doubt, round up: a unit straining at its limit during a humidity spike is a unit that fails exactly when you need it most.

Corrections Most Calculators Skip

Ceiling height. Charts assume standard 8-foot ceilings. Taller ceilings mean more air volume holding more moisture — scale the requirement by ceiling height ÷ 8. A 10-foot ceiling adds 25 percent. Moisture sources each add load: a washer/dryer, an aquarium, a jungle of houseplants, or new construction still drying out (building materials release moisture for months). This tool adds 2 pints per active source. Temperature is the quiet killer: below about 60°F, standard refrigerant dehumidifiers lose efficiency fast and can ice up. For cold basements and crawl spaces, choose a desiccant or low-temperature-rated (LGR) unit instead of just buying bigger — and the tool flags this explicitly rather than silently oversizing you.

Round Up to a Real Size

Dehumidifiers are sold in fixed capacities — 20, 30, 50, 70, 90+ pints — so the final step is rounding your computed need up to the next available unit. A calculated 24 pints becomes a 30-pint unit; a calculated 52 becomes 70. Never round down to save money: the price gap between adjacent sizes is small, while the performance gap during a wet week is enormous. If your space falls at the very top of a unit's rated range, jump a size — ratings describe ideal conditions, and your basement in August is not ideal conditions.

Where to Put It and How to Drain It

Place the unit centrally with at least 6 to 12 inches of clearance on all sides — it needs airflow to work. Keep interior doors open so it can treat the whole space; a dehumidifier locked in a closed room only dries that room. For drainage, a gravity hose to a floor drain or sink beats emptying a bucket every day — buckets fill at the worst times and the unit shuts off when full, which is exactly when humidity spikes. If no drain is nearby, a condensate pump (a small add-on pump) lifts water to a distant sink or outside. Elevate the unit slightly if the drain is far; gravity needs a downhill run.

Target Humidity and What It Prevents

Keep indoor relative humidity between 30 and 50 percent. Below 30, air feels dry, wood shrinks and static builds; above 50, dust mites thrive; above 60, mold grows on any organic surface — drywall, wood, fabric. The musty smell in a damp basement is microbial volatile organic compounds: mold announcing itself. A correctly sized dehumidifier holding 45 percent does more for a basement's health than any air freshener, and it protects stored belongings, prevents efflorescence damage on masonry, and stops the clammy feeling that makes below-grade rooms unpleasant.

Energy Use and Running Cost

Dehumidifiers are among the thirstier small appliances: a 30-pint unit draws roughly 300 to 400 watts while running. At 12 hours a day and average US electricity rates, that is on the order of $15 to $25 a month — the tool's cost tab computes it from your actual inputs. Three things cut the bill: buy ENERGY STAR (about 15 percent more efficient for the same moisture removal), fix the moisture source (seal foundation cracks, extend downspouts, ventilate the dryer outside), and do not oversize wildly — a correctly sized unit reaches target humidity and cycles off, while a monster unit short-cycles without ever settling. In shoulder seasons, a humidistat (built into most modern units) is worth its weight: set it to 45 percent and let the machine decide when to run.

When a Dehumidifier Is Not the Answer

Some problems outrank any portable unit. Active water intrusion — water flowing in during rain — needs waterproofing, grading and drainage first; a dehumidifier cannot bail a boat. Condensation on single-pane windows in winter is a ventilation and insulation problem. And a whole house that is humid everywhere is better served by a whole-house dehumidifier tied into the HVAC than by three portable units humming in three rooms. The portable unit shines in exactly one scenario: a defined damp space (basement, crawl space, laundry room) with no active leak, where steady moisture removal holds the line at 45 percent.

Sizing for Special Spaces

Not every damp space is a basement. Crawl spaces are the hardest case: low, cold, and often dirt-floored, they need a low-temperature unit and usually a sealed vapor barrier on the ground — dehumidifying an unsealed dirt crawl space is like heating a house with the windows open. Size for the footprint, then add 15 percent for the cold. Laundry rooms spike hard but briefly; a 20 to 30 pint unit with a humidistat handles the bursts if the dryer vents outside — if it vents inside, fix that first, because no portable unit outruns a dryer dumping gallons of moisture into a small room. Garages and workshops in humid climates do well with a mid-size unit near the most moisture-sensitive storage (tools rust long before you notice the humidity). Indoor grow spaces are a specialty of their own: transpiring plants dump enormous moisture, so size 25 to 30 percent above the calculated load and expect to run near-continuously during late flowering. In every special case the method is the same — honest dampness rating, real square footage, temperature correction, round up — only the context changes.

Maintenance: Five Minutes a Month

Clean or replace the air filter monthly — a clogged filter is the number one cause of weak performance and icing. Wipe the coils yearly, check that the drain hose runs downhill and is not kinked, and empty and disinfect the bucket if you use one (stagnant water grows its own ecosystem). In winter, a basement unit may need its humidistat raised or the unit stored; running a refrigerant dehumidifier in a near-freezing space just makes ice. Five minutes of maintenance preserves both capacity and lifespan, and it is the difference between a unit that lasts three years and one that lasts ten.

Frequently asked questions

What size dehumidifier do I need for my basement?

Match square footage to dampness level: a 500 sq ft moderately damp space needs ~10 pints/day, very damp ~12, wet ~14. Add ~4–5 pints per extra 500 sq ft, scale up for ceilings over 8 ft, and round up to the next real unit size (20/30/50/70). The calculator above does all of this from your inputs.

Does a dehumidifier work in a cold basement?

Yes. Below ~60°F, standard refrigerant units lose efficiency rapidly and can ice up, because cold air holds less moisture. For cold basements or crawl spaces, choose a desiccant or low-temperature-rated (LGR) dehumidifier instead of just buying a bigger standard unit.

Is it better to oversize a dehumidifier?

No — buy with ~25–30% headroom above your calculated need and round up to the next standard size, but don't wildly oversize. An oversized unit short-cycles (rapid on/off), wasting energy and shortening compressor life without controlling humidity better.

What humidity level should I set my dehumidifier to?

Aim for 30–50% relative humidity — 45% is the sweet spot for most homes. Below 30% air feels dry and wood shrinks; above 60% mold grows and dust mites thrive. The musty basement smell is mold's volatile compounds, a sign you're above the safe range.

How much electricity does a dehumidifier use?

A 30-pint unit draws ~300–400W while running. At 12 hours/day and average electricity rates that's roughly $15–$25/month. ENERGY STAR models use ~15% less for the same moisture removal. Use the running-cost tab above with your wattage, hours and local rate for an exact figure.

How do I maintain a dehumidifier?

Clean the filter monthly, verify the drain hose runs downhill without kinks, and wipe the coils yearly. If you use the bucket, empty and disinfect it regularly. Don't run a refrigerant unit in near-freezing spaces — it will just ice up.
