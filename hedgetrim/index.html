About HedgeTrim

Why hedge jobs take longer than expected

A hedge that looks like an hour's work from the garden often takes three, because the visible face is only part of the job: the top, the far side, the ends, and the cleanup all add time, and overgrown hedges hide thick woody stems that slow every cut. Planning with a time estimate changes the day: you know whether to start after breakfast or to book a whole Saturday, whether one person can manage it, and whether a contractor's quote is reasonable. This calculator estimates cutting time from hedge length, height and density, and converts the cut volume into green waste bags, so the job, the help and the disposal are all planned before the first cut.

How the time and waste figures work

Cutting time scales with the area of hedge face, length times height, at roughly 0.08 hours per square metre for a light annual trim, multiplied by 1.5 for dense or overgrown growth. That rate assumes powered shears and a reasonably accessible hedge; hand shears, ladders and awkward access all add time. Waste bags are estimated at 0.15 bags per square metre of hedge face, rounded up, since trimmings compress poorly and a bag that looks full still settles. These are planning figures, not guarantees: a freshly trimmed box hedge goes much faster than a neglected leylandii of the same size, and the estimate should be treated as a starting point to adjust from experience.

When to trim each hedge type

Timing matters for both the hedge and wildlife. Most formal hedges are trimmed in late spring and again in late summer, after the main growth flush and before autumn. Flowering hedges should be trimmed after flowering, so you do not remove next year's buds. Always check for nesting birds before cutting between March and August, since disturbing an active nest is illegal in many places and distressing everywhere; if birds are nesting, delay that section until the young have fledged. Avoid trimming in frost, which damages fresh cuts, and in extreme heat, which stresses the plant.

Tools: shears, trimmers and ladders

Hand shears give the neatest finish on small and formal hedges such as box, and they are quiet and precise, but they are slow on long runs. Powered hedge trimmers, electric or petrol, transform large hedges, with petrol models suiting remote and very large hedges and electric models suiting most gardens. Keep blades sharp and clean, since blunt blades tear rather than cut, leaving brown edges and entry points for disease. Ladders need a stable base and a second person footing them for tall hedges, and a tripod orchard ladder is far safer on uneven ground than a standard ladder. Never overreach: move the ladder instead.

Cutting technique for a healthy hedge

Cut with the blade angled slightly so the top of the hedge is narrower than the base, a shape called batter, which lets light reach the lower branches and prevents the base from thinning out. Work from the bottom up on the sides, and from one end to the other on the top, keeping the cut line level by eye or with a string line stretched between stakes. Remove dead wood and crossing branches as you go, and step back regularly to check the overall shape, since it is easy to carve a dip that only shows from a distance. For overgrown hedges, restore shape over two or three seasons rather than cutting hard in one go, which can shock the plant.

Cleanup and green waste

Clear trimmings as you go rather than leaving a mountain at the end, since cut material left on lawns kills grass underneath within days. A tarp laid under the hedge catches most clippings and makes collection a single drag rather than hours of raking. Compost soft trimmings, but check whether woody material needs shredding first, and never compost diseased material, which should go in green waste collection instead. Bag counts from the calculator help here: order the right number of bags or book a green waste collection before starting, so the garden is tidy the same day the hedge is cut.

Safety essentials

Eye protection is essential, since trimmings fly unpredictably, and gloves protect against thorns, blisters and bladed tools. Wear sturdy footwear, long sleeves and hearing protection with petrol trimmers, and keep cables behind you when using electric tools, so you never cut through your own lead. Check for overhead power lines before starting, and hire a professional for any hedge near them, since electricity and long metal tools are a lethal combination. Keep children and pets away from the working area, and take breaks: fatigue causes most hedge-trimming injuries, which happen late in a long job rather than at the start.

Hiring a contractor versus doing it yourself

Contractors charge by time, hedge size and disposal, so the calculator's time and bag figures give you a fair basis for comparing quotes: a quote far above the estimated time should be questioned, while a very low quote may exclude disposal. Ask whether the price includes waste removal, whether the contractor is insured, and whether they will check for nesting birds. Doing it yourself saves money and gives a satisfying result, but a tall, overgrown or boundary hedge may be better left to professionals with the right ladders and insurance. Either way, a written agreement on scope and cleanup prevents disputes.

Aftercare for a freshly cut hedge

Water the hedge after trimming in dry weather, since cutting exposes inner growth to sun and wind, and mulch the base to retain moisture. Feed in spring with a balanced fertiliser if growth is weak, but avoid feeding late in the season, which encourages soft growth that frost damages. Watch for brown edges in the days after cutting, which usually indicate blunt blades or cutting in hot sun, and trim lightly again once the hedge recovers. A well-cut hedge thickens over the season, and regular light trims keep it dense, while infrequent hard cuts leave it thin and woody. Consistency is the secret to a hedge that looks good all year.

Restoring a neglected hedge

A hedge neglected for years cannot be restored in a single hard cut without risking its health, so plan a two to three season renovation. In the first year, cut one side and the top lightly, removing dead wood and shaping the outline, and let the hedge recover through the growing season. In the second year, cut the other side and reduce height gradually, never removing more than a third of the foliage in one season for most species. Conifers are the exception that proves the rule: yew and privet regenerate from old wood, but leylandii and other conifers do not regrow from bare brown wood, so a conifer cut back to brown stems will stay brown permanently. For such hedges, regular light trimming is the only maintenance that works, and replacement may be kinder than renovation. Feed and water a renovated hedge well, mulch the base, and be patient: a hedge restored gradually over three years looks far better than one shocked by a single drastic cut, and it keeps its role as a wildlife shelter throughout the process.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to trim a hedge?

Roughly 0.08 hours per m² of hedge face for a light trim — a 20 m × 1.5 m hedge takes about 2.4 hours.

How many waste bags will hedge trimmings fill?

About 0.15 bags per m² of hedge face, rounded up — plan disposal before you start.

When is the best time to trim a hedge?

Late spring and late summer for most formal hedges; after flowering for flowering types; always check for nesting birds first.

Should a hedge be wider at the bottom?

Yes — cut with a slight batter so light reaches lower branches and the base stays dense.

Can I trim a hedge near power lines myself?

No — hire a professional for any hedge near overhead lines.

How do I keep a hedge dense?

Trim lightly and regularly, keep the base wider than the top, and avoid hard cuts on overgrown hedges in a single season.
