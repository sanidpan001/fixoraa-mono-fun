The Riff Comes First — Theory Comes Second

Almost nobody writes music by thinking “I would like to compose in D dorian today.” The real workflow runs backwards: you are noodling on the guitar, you land on a handful of notes that sound right together, the riff is good — and you have no idea what scale you are actually in. Without a name for it, you cannot extend the riff, you cannot solo over it with confidence, and you cannot tell the bass player what to play. That gap between “this sounds right” and “this is in such-and-such” is exactly what a reverse scale finder closes.

Traditional scale tools go one direction: pick a scale, see the notes. That is useful for practice, but it is the opposite of how songs get written. ScaleSpot goes the other way — you feed it the notes you are already playing, and it searches 96 scales (eight scale types across all twelve roots) to find every scale that contains them, ranked so the tightest fit lands on top. It is the direction you actually write in.

How the Matching Works

The logic is simple set matching, and understanding it makes you a better user. Your notes are converted to pitch classes (C is C no matter the octave), then each of the 96 scales is checked: does this scale contain all of your notes? Every scale that passes becomes a candidate. Candidates are ranked by extra notes — how many scale tones you did not play. A five-note riff that exactly fills a pentatonic box (zero extra notes, 100% fit) outranks a seven-note scale that merely contains your five notes, because the tighter scale describes your riff more precisely.

This ranking mirrors how musicians actually think. If your riff uses C, D, E, G and A, calling it “C major” is technically true but musically vague — C major contains two notes you never touched. Calling it “C major pentatonic” (or its twin, A minor pentatonic) tells you exactly where you live. The fit meter on each result card makes this visible: 100% means your notes are the scale; lower percentages mean the scale is a bigger house with empty rooms.

The Eight Scale Types in the Database

Major (1 2 3 4 5 6 7) is the bright, resolved default — the “do re mi” sound. Natural minor (1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7) is its dark sibling, the classic emotional key of countless ballads and metal anthems. These two seven-note parents anchor everything else.

Major pentatonic (1 2 3 5 6) and minor pentatonic (1 b3 4 5 b7) are the five-note workhorses. The minor pentatonic is the single most-played scale in rock guitar — the famous “box” shape. If your riff is four or five notes and sounds like rock, the answer is very often a pentatonic. Blues (1 b3 4 b5 5 b7) adds the flat-five “blue note” to the minor pentatonic; that one chromatic tone is the cry in the voice of blues and hard rock.

The three modes cover the in-between colors: dorian (1 2 b3 4 5 6 b7) is minor with a raised sixth — the Santana and funk sound; mixolydian (1 2 3 4 5 6 b7) is major with a flat seventh — the swagger of blues-rock and folk-rock; harmonic minor (1 2 b3 4 5 b6 7) raises the seventh for an exotic, tense pull back home — the sound of metal leads and Middle Eastern-flavored melodies.

Reading Your Results Like a Musician

Start with the best fit card, but do not stop there. The second and third results are often relatives of the first — A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic contain the exact same notes, just centered on different roots. That is not a bug; it is music theory telling you that your riff has two valid homes. The practical question is: where does the riff resolve? If phrases keep landing on A, think A minor; if they land on C, think C major. The tool gives you the candidates; your ear picks the winner.

The formula line (e.g. 1 b3 4 5 b7) is worth learning to read. It describes each scale as alterations of the major scale, and once you can read formulas you can build any scale from any root without a tool. The mood note on each card is a one-line character sketch — useful when you are choosing between two close matches for a songwriting decision.

For major and natural minor results, the chords in key strip is pure gold. It shows the seven diatonic triads with their Roman numerals — the chord palette your riff was born from. Writing a chorus? Start with the I, IV and V. Need a sadder verse? Borrow from the vi or ii. This is the bridge from “what scale is this” to “what do I play next.”

What to Do With the Answer

A scale name is only useful if it changes what your hands do. Here is the playbook:

  1. Extend the riff. The result card shows the full scale — the highlighted notes are yours, the rest are the notes you have not tried yet. Those unplayed scale tones are your safest melodic extensions.
  2. Solo over it. Play the matching pentatonic or full scale over the riff. Because every note belongs to the same family, nearly everything you play will sound intentional.
  3. Write the chords. Use the harmonized chords strip to build a progression under the riff. In major, I–V–vi–IV fits almost anything; in minor, try i–VI–III–VII.
  4. Talk to the band. “It is in A minor pentatonic” is a complete musical instruction. The bassist, keyboardist and second guitarist instantly know their note pool.
  5. Transpose it. Once you know the scale type, you can move the whole riff to any root and keep its character — handy when the singer needs it lower.

Common Traps (and How to Dodge Them)

The chromatic passing tone. The number-one reason a search returns nothing is a single note that does not belong — a bluesy flat-five slipped into a major riff, or a chromatic walk-up between chord tones. The tool tells you when no scale contains all your notes; the fix is to drop the odd one out and re-run. Passing tones are decoration, not residents.

Key vs. scale confusion. “What key is this in?” and “what scale is this?” are cousins, not twins. A song in the key of C major might use C major pentatonic for the verse riff and C mixolydian for the solo — same key, different scales. ScaleSpot answers the scale question; the key is usually the chord the song resolves to.

Too few notes. Two notes match dozens of scales — the ranking cannot help you because nearly everything fits. Give the tool at least four distinct notes; five to seven is the sweet spot where the best fit genuinely means something.

Enharmonic blindness. Bb and A# are the same pitch. The sharps/flats toggle exists so the results speak your instrument's language — flat keys (F, Bb, Eb) read naturally in flats, sharp keys (G, D, A, E) in sharps. The matching is identical either way.

Practice Drills Using Your Results

Turn the tool into a teacher with three drills. Drill 1 — Fill the box: take your best-fit pentatonic and play it ascending and descending across all six strings, then improvise for two minutes using only those five notes. Constraint breeds melody. Drill 2 — Find the relatives: play your riff's scale, then its relative major/minor (the tool's second result is often the relative) back to back. Hear how the same notes change character when the root moves. Drill 3 — Chord-tone targeting: loop the I–IV–V from the harmonized strip and force yourself to land each chord change on a chord tone from the scale. This is the single fastest path from “playing scales” to “playing music.”

Why This Beats Guessing (or Asking a Forum)

Posting “what scale is C D E G A” on a forum gets you five confident, contradictory answers. The math does not argue: subset matching across a fixed, correct database gives you every valid answer, ranked by a transparent rule, in under a second. And unlike a forum, it shows its work — the fit meter, the formulas and the chord strips let you verify the answer with your own ears. Use the tool for speed, then use the drills above to make the knowledge yours. The goal was never the label; it is what the label lets you play next.

Frequently asked questions

How do I use a reverse scale finder?

Tap every distinct note in your riff or melody on the note deck — order doesn't matter. ScaleSpot checks all 96 scales in its database (8 scale types × 12 roots) and lists every scale containing your notes, ranked by fewest extra notes. The top card is your best fit, with the scale's notes, formula, mood and harmonized chords.

How many notes do I need to enter to identify a scale?

Four distinct notes is the practical minimum; five to seven is the sweet spot. With only two or three notes, dozens of scales match and the ranking can't help you. If your riff is short, add the notes from the chord underneath it — that usually completes the picture.

What should I do if no scale matches my notes?

You almost certainly have a chromatic passing tone — one note that decorates the riff but doesn't live in the scale, like a bluesy flat-five in a major riff. Remove the most suspicious note and re-run. The tool says so explicitly when the note set matches nothing.

What is the difference between A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic in my results?

They contain the exact same five notes — they're relatives. The difference is the tonal center: if your phrases resolve to A, think A minor; if they resolve to C, think C major. Seeing both in your results isn't a contradiction, it's theory telling you your riff has two valid homes.

Can I use the results to write chords for my riff?

Yes — that's what the 'Chords in key' strip is for. For major and natural minor results it shows all seven diatonic triads with Roman numerals. Start with I, IV and V for a chorus, borrow the vi or ii for a sadder verse, and you'll stay inside the riff's harmonic world.

Does the sharps/flats toggle change the results?

No — matching is done on pitch classes, so Bb and A# are identical to the engine. The toggle only changes how note names are displayed, so flat keys like F and Bb read naturally in flats and sharp keys like E and A read in sharps.
