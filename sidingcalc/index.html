Measuring Wall by Wall

The number-one siding estimating mistake is measuring the house as one big rectangle. Real houses have gables, bump-outs, porches, and different wall heights — and a single-rectangle estimate is wrong in ways that always cost money. The professional method is a wall-by-wall takeoff: each wall gets its own width × height, its own openings, and then you add the nets.

Measure width along the foundation and height from the foundation top to the eave (or to the gable peak for gable ends — approximate the triangle as half of width × gable height and add it to that wall). Measure in feet and do not fuss over inches; the waste allowance absorbs small errors, but it cannot absorb a forgotten gable.

Walk the house with a tape and a notepad, one wall at a time, and enter each above. Two minutes per wall buys an order you can trust — and the habit of measuring twice transfers to every project after this one.

Deducting Openings Right

Every window and door is siding you do not buy — but only if you deduct it. The standard deductions are 21 square feet per door (3×7) and 15 square feet per window (3×5), which this calculator applies automatically from your counts. For picture windows or sliding doors, bump the deduction mentally; for tiny basement windows, do not bother.

Here is the nuance beginners miss: you deduct the opening’s area, but you add trim around it. Every opening needs J-channel on all four sides to receive the siding — roughly 16 feet per window and 17 per door. The calculator estimates that trim for you, because an order with perfect siding and no J-channel is a stalled job.

Do not deduct for anything under about 4 square feet (small vents, hose bibs) — the cutting waste around them eats the savings. And never deduct the garage door as a plain rectangle without checking: an 8×7 garage door is 56 square feet, worth deducting, but its perimeter trim adds up too.

Squares and Boxes

Siding is sold like roofing: by the square (100 sq ft) and by the box. A standard box of vinyl siding covers 2 squares (200 sq ft) — packaging varies a little by manufacturer, but 2 squares per box is the planning number the industry uses, and it is what this calculator assumes.

The math: net wall area ÷ 100 = squares; squares ÷ 2, rounded up = boxes. Rounding up matters because you cannot buy half a box, and the round-up is part of your buffer. A 14.2-square job orders 8 boxes (16 squares of material) — that extra 1.8 squares is not waste, it is insurance against miscuts and dye-lot roulette.

Fiber cement and engineered wood sell differently — often by the piece or by the square foot — but the square remains the common language. Convert your takeoff to squares first and every supplier can quote you apples to apples.

Waste and Why 10%

Waste in siding is cut-offs: the ends trimmed at corners, the pieces notched around windows, the occasional miscut. On simple rectangular walls, 5–10 percent covers it. Add gables, angled walls, or lots of windows and step up to 15 percent — every angle multiplies cutting.

Panel length interacts with waste in a way people miss. Vinyl comes in 12-foot (sometimes 12.5-foot) lengths; a 9-foot wall wastes 3 feet per course unless you plan joints. Stagger joints randomly (never aligned vertically — that looks amateur and leaks) and the offcuts from one course start the next, which is how pros keep waste near the low end.

Keep a full box after the job. Siding fades, colors get discontinued, and a storm-damaged panel replaced from your own stock matches far better than anything you will find in five years.

Trim: J-Channel, Starter, Corners

Trim is the difference between a siding job and a siding installation. The list: starter strip along the bottom of every wall (the calculator totals your wall widths for this), J-channel around every opening and along the top under the eave, corner posts (inside and outside) for every vertical corner, and utility trim under windowsills.

Count corners by walking the house: every outside corner gets an outside corner post, every inside corner an inside post. They come in 10-foot lengths — divide your corner heights accordingly and round up. Underside of eaves (soffit) and the fascia board are separate materials; do not expect the siding order to cover them.

Order trim in the same color and lot as the siding. Trim pieces from a different run can be visibly off-shade, and trim is exactly where the eye goes — around windows and along corners.

Vinyl vs Fiber Cement Math

Vinyl is the budget king: cheap per square, DIY-friendly, and the box math above applies directly. Its weakness is impact damage and fading on dark colors; its strength is that any homeowner can install it with basic tools.

Fiber cement (the Hardie-style boards) costs 2–3× more per square, weighs far more, and needs specialized cutting (dust control is a health requirement, not a suggestion) — but it holds paint 2–3× longer and shrugs off hail. Waste runs higher, around 10–15 percent, because miscuts are expensive and boards are heavy to handle.

Engineered wood sits between: lighter than fiber cement, more rigid than vinyl, prefinished options that look excellent. Whichever you choose, run the same wall-by-wall takeoff — the material changes, the geometry does not.

Ordering and Dye Lots

Siding color varies slightly between manufacturing runs — dye lots. Two boxes from different lots can show a faint line across the wall, visible forever. The defense: order everything at once from one supplier and ask for a single lot. Suppliers do this routinely if you ask.

Check every box on delivery before the truck leaves: right color, right profile, no crushed ends. Vinyl panels are long and fragile; a cracked interlock edge is a leak path. Refuse damaged boxes on the spot — reordering one box later means a different lot.

Store boxes flat, out of the sun, and install within a few weeks. Vinyl expands and contracts with temperature — install it in moderate weather when you can, and never nail it tight. The nail hem has slots for a reason: the panel must hang and slide.

DIY or Contractor

Vinyl siding is genuinely DIY-able on a single-story, simple-geometry house — the tools are a snap-lock punch, tin snips, a level, and patience. Budget roughly double the time you think for the first wall; the learning curve is real but the skill is forgiving.

Call a contractor for two stories (ladder and staging safety), fiber cement (weight and silica dust), or any wall with rot or structural questions underneath. Get three quotes, compare them per square, and ask each bidder what their takeoff was — a bidder whose square count matches yours within 10 percent did the homework.

Either way, your wall-by-wall takeoff is leverage. You will spot a padded material line, you will know whether the trim is included, and you will order with confidence instead of hoping the supplier’s guess was right.

One final tip whatever path you choose: photograph each wall before the old siding comes off, noting where outlets, lights, and hose bibs sit. Those photos become the map for mounting blocks and trim placement on the way back up — and they settle every "was that always there?" argument with the crew. Measure twice, photograph once, and the siding job practically manages itself.

Frequently asked questions

How much siding do I need for a 2000 sq ft house?

A 2,000 sq ft two-story home typically has 2,200–2,800 sq ft of wall area after deducting openings — roughly 22–28 squares, or 11–14 boxes of vinyl plus 10% waste. Gables and wall height swing it a lot, so measure wall by wall above for your exact number.

How do you calculate siding square footage?

Measure each wall’s width × height, subtract 21 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window, add the walls together, then add 10% waste. Divide by 100 for squares; divide squares by 2 and round up for boxes of vinyl siding.

How many boxes of siding do I need?

One box of vinyl siding covers about 2 squares (200 sq ft). Take your net wall square footage, add waste, divide by 200, and round up. A 2,000-sq-ft wall job needs roughly 11–14 boxes depending on openings and waste.

What is a square of siding?

One square equals 100 square feet of wall coverage. Siding, like roofing, is estimated and often priced by the square. Vinyl siding is typically boxed 2 squares per box.

Do I need to subtract windows and doors when ordering siding?

Yes — deduct 21 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window from each wall. But remember to add J-channel trim around every opening (about 16 ft per window, 17 ft per door), which the calculator estimates for you.

How much extra siding should I order for waste?

10% is standard for straightforward walls; 15% for gables, angled walls, or window-heavy walls. The waste covers cut-offs at corners and openings plus handling damage. Keep one full box after the job for future repairs.
