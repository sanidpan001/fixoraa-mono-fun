The 3× Rule: Depth in One Sentence

Plant a bulb three times as deep as the bulb is tall, measuring from the soil surface to the top of the bulb — or equivalently, dig the hole about three bulb-heights and set the bulb on the bottom. A 2-inch tulip bulb goes 6 inches deep; a 1-inch crocus corm goes 3 inches deep; a 3-inch allium goes 8 or 9 inches down. This single rule covers nearly every common bulb, and it exists for good reasons: depth insulates against temperature swings, anchors tall stems against wind, keeps bulbs below the thirsty root zone of summer annuals, and hides them from squirrels.

The exceptions prove the rule. Daffodils tolerate deeper planting (it delays division and extends their perennial life). Lilies like extra depth because they root along the buried stem. Garlic and shallow wildflowers break the rule downward. But when a bulb is not in this calculator's database, 3× height is the guess that is most often right.

Why Depth Matters More Than You Think

A bulb is a lunchbox: everything the spring shoot needs is packed inside, and depth decides how safely that lunchbox sits through winter. Too shallow, and freeze-thaw cycles heave the bulb to the surface, where it desiccates or becomes squirrel breakfast. Shallow bulbs also produce short, floppy stems — the classic "my tulips bloomed at ankle height" complaint — because the stem never had the soil resistance that triggers sturdy elongation.

Too deep is rarer and more forgiving: an over-deep bulb simply emerges late and may skip a year while it pulls itself up via contractile roots (yes, bulbs can move themselves). The asymmetry is the lesson — when uncertain, err deeper. The calculator's depths already include that bias.

Spacing: The Drift vs. The Specimen

Spacing serves two masters: this year's display and the bulbs' future. Tight spacing (2 bulb-widths) gives the lush, professional drift look in year one but forces division sooner as bulbs crowd. Wide spacing (3–4 bulb-widths) looks sparse initially yet lets daffodils and crocuses naturalize into multiplying colonies that improve for a decade.

The per-square-foot numbers in the calculator assume display spacing — what you want for a bed that should look full this spring. For naturalizing areas under trees or in lawns, plant at 1.5× the listed spacing and let time do the thickening. And remember the design trick: odd-numbered clusters (5, 7, 9) read as natural; even rows read as municipal.

Timing: The Soil-Temperature Trigger

Fall bulbs want in when soil drops below 60°F — typically October into November in most temperate zones. Plant too early in warm soil and bulbs sprout prematurely, wasting energy; plant after the ground freezes and you cannot dig. The month strips in the calculator show each bulb's window, but soil temperature beats the calendar: a $10 soil thermometer removes the guesswork.

Summer bloomers invert the schedule: gladiolus, dahlias, and lilies go in after frost danger passes, when soil is workably warm — April to May for most zones. In warm-winter climates (zones 9–10), fall bulbs need artificial chilling: 8–10 weeks in the refrigerator (never near fruit, whose ethylene gas damages flower buds) before planting.

Planting Technique That Pays Off

Dig the hole or trench to the full depth, loosen the bottom inch of soil, and set each bulb pointy-end up on undisturbed — not fluffy — soil so it does not sink deeper after watering. Backfill with the excavated soil; contrary to old advice, rich compost in the hole is unnecessary and can rot the basal plate in wet winters. A pinch of bone meal is traditional and harmless, but trials show it matters little in decent soil.

Water once, deeply, after planting — even in fall rain — to settle soil around the bulb and trigger root growth before winter. Then mulch 2–3 inches after the ground freezes (not before, which invites rodents to nest). Mark the planting area: the number-one bulb killer is the spring gardener's trowel, and a few discreet markers prevent friendly fire.

Pointy End Up (and Other Orientation Truths)

The pointed end holds the shoot; the flat basal plate grows roots. Planted upside down, most bulbs detect gravity and curve the shoot around — costing weeks of stored energy and often producing a blind (flowerless) shoot. Sideways planting is nearly harmless; the shoot corrects quickly. If a bulb is ambiguous (some alliums and anemones are just lumps), plant it sideways and let biology sort it out.

After the Bloom: The Part Everyone Skips

The six weeks after flowering decide next year's show. Deadhead spent blooms so energy goes to the bulb, not seeds — but leave every leaf untouched until it yellows fully. Those floppy leaves are solar panels recharging the bulb; braiding or tying them reduces photosynthesis and starves next year's flower. Hide the yellowing foliage behind emerging perennials rather than cutting it.

Do not water dormant summer bulbs in heavy soil — wet dormancy rots them. Do lift and divide only when clumps bloom poorly or produce mostly leaves: daffodils every 4–5 years, tulips rarely (most modern tulips are treated as annuals in warm zones), crocuses when congested. Replant divisions immediately at the proper depth; bulbs dry out faster than you expect.

Troubleshooting: Blinds, Flops, and Vanishings

Leaves but no flowers ("blind" bulbs) usually means shallow planting, premature foliage removal last year, or overcrowding — in that order. Floppy stems point to shallow planting or too much shade. Bulbs that vanish entirely were likely eaten (squirrels, voles) or rotted in waterlogged soil; plant vulnerable varieties in wire mesh baskets and improve drainage with grit. Flowers that shrink yearly signal nutrient exhaustion or crowding — feed lightly with balanced fertilizer as shoots emerge, or divide.

Keep notes — which variety, where, how deep, how it performed. That is what this notebook page is for. Three springs of notes turn bulb planting from an annual gamble into a refined craft, and the gardener with notes always out-blooms the gardener with memory.

Forcing Bulbs Indoors

The same depth rules apply when you force bulbs for winter bloom, with one twist: pots need less soil below the bulb but the same cover above it. Choose a pot with drainage, fill the bottom third with gritty mix, set bulbs shoulder to shoulder (they like company in pots), and cover with mix so the tips just peek out. Then comes the non-negotiable step — 10 to 14 weeks of cold (35–48°F) in a fridge, cold garage, or buried outdoors. Without vernalization there are no flowers, only leaves. Paperwhites and amaryllis skip the chill entirely, which is why they dominate holiday forcing. After bloom, forced bulbs are spent for indoor purposes; plant them outdoors at proper depth and they may recover in a year or two.

Deer, Squirrels, and Other Critics

Depth hides bulbs from squirrels only partly — a determined squirrel excavates anything. The reliable defenses are daffodils, alliums, and fritillaria, which contain compounds rodents and deer dislike; interplant them among the vulnerable tulips and crocuses as bodyguards. Wire mesh laid flat over the bed at planting time stops digging cold (lift it once shoots emerge). For deer, the only real answers are fencing or unpalatable varieties — repellent sprays wash off and need relentless reapplication. And remember: the depth in your notebook matters here too. A tulip at a full 6 inches survives casual squirrel interest; one at 3 inches is takeout.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should I plant tulip bulbs?

Plant tulips 6 inches deep — about three times the bulb's height — and 4 to 5 inches apart, in October or November. The 3x rule works for most bulbs: measure the bulb's height and plant three times that deep.

What happens if you plant bulbs too shallow?

Shallow bulbs freeze in winter, dry out in spring, flop over under their own flowers, and get eaten by squirrels. If blooms are short and floppy, depth is the first suspect. You cannot fix it without replanting at the right depth.

Which end of the bulb goes up?

Pointy end up, flat basal plate down where the roots were. Most bulbs will right themselves if planted sideways, but upside-down planting wastes the bulb's energy and delays or prevents blooming.

How far apart should bulbs be spaced?

Roughly 2 to 3 bulb-widths apart: tulips and daffodils 4–6 inches, crocuses 3 inches, big alliums 8 inches. Closer spacing gives a lush drift look; wider spacing lets bulbs naturalize and multiply for years.

When is the best month to plant bulbs?

Fall bulbs (tulips, daffodils, crocuses, alliums) go in September through November, once soil cools below 60°F. Summer bloomers (gladiolus, dahlias, lilies) plant in April–May after frost danger passes. The calculator shows each bulb's window.

How many bulbs do I need for a square foot?

It depends on spacing: crocuses at 3-inch spacing need about 16 per square foot, tulips at 5 inches need about 6, and big dahlias need just 1. Enter your bed size in the calculator for an exact shopping number.
