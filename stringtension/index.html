What tennis string tension actually does

String tension is one of the most talked-about settings in tennis, and one of the most misunderstood. The number, usually quoted in pounds or kilograms, is the tension the stringing machine pulls each string to while your racket is strung. It shapes how the ball leaves the strings: lower tension generally gives a livelier, more powerful response with a softer feel, while higher tension gives a firmer, more controlled response with a smaller margin for error. The difference is real but subtle, a few pounds either way will not transform your game, and the right tension is the one that lets you swing confidently without spraying balls long or feeling harsh vibration in the arm.

Two things confuse the picture. First, strings lose tension from the moment they are strung, fastest in the first day and then gradually, so a racket strung at 55 pounds may play at noticeably less within a week or two, depending on string type and how much you play. Second, the feel of a string bed depends on the whole system: string material, gauge, racket head size, and string pattern all interact. A stiff polyester at high tension in a small head feels very different from a soft multifilament at the same tension in a large head. This calculator helps you reason about one part of that system: how head size shifts the effective tension you feel, so you can translate a tension that worked in one racket to a different racket sensibly.

Head size, pattern and gauge

Larger head sizes have longer strings, and longer strings deflect more under the same pull, which is why the same reference tension feels softer in an oversize head than in a midsize one. The calculator applies a small adjustment for head size around a 100 square inch reference, so a tension that felt right in a 100 head can be nudged up for a 110 head if you want the same firmness. String pattern matters too: an open pattern with fewer strings, such as 16 by 19, gives more string movement, more spin potential, and a livelier feel, while a dense pattern such as 18 by 20 gives a firmer, more controlled bed. Gauge works in the same direction: thinner strings bite the ball more and feel livelier, thicker strings last longer and feel firmer.

Material is the biggest feel lever of all. Natural gut is soft, powerful and arm-friendly but expensive and weather-sensitive. Multifilament strings mimic gut's softness at lower cost. Polyester strings are stiff, durable and spin-friendly, and dominate modern pro setups, but they transmit more shock and lose playability as they go dead, so they suit players who restring often and swing fast. Hybrids, polyester mains with a softer cross string, try to combine spin and comfort. If you have arm pain, the first changes to consider are a softer string, a slightly lower tension, and a thicker, more shock-absorbing grip, before chasing a new racket.

Choosing a starting tension

Most rackets print a recommended tension range on the frame, and that range is an excellent starting point. Beginners and players who want more free power usually do well at the lower end of the range, while advanced players with fast, full swings who generate their own power often prefer the middle or upper end for control. If your balls consistently fly long, try two or three pounds higher; if they fall short or your arm feels jarred, try two or three pounds lower. Change one variable at a time, string type or tension, never both, or you will not know what helped. Keep a note of what you used, when it was strung, and how it felt after a few sessions, because memory for string feel fades fast.

Restringing frequency matters as much as the number. A good rule is to restring as many times a year as you play per week: three sessions a week means about three restrings a year, more for polyester users. Dead strings feel dull, lose control, and increase arm stress, and no tension adjustment rescues a string bed that has gone dead. If you break strings often, a thicker gauge or a more durable material saves money and hassle. If you rarely break them, prioritise feel and comfort over durability, since a string that lasts forever but feels harsh is a poor bargain for your arm and your game.

A practical translation example

Suppose you loved 55 pounds in a 100 square inch racket and switch to a 110 head. The longer strings will make the same 55 feel softer, so the calculator suggests around 56 pounds to restore a similar firmness. That is a starting point for your next stringing, not a law: try it, play three or four sessions, and adjust by two pounds in either direction based on whether balls fly long or fall short. Over a season, this patient, small-step approach builds a setup you trust, which is worth far more than copying a professional's tension. Pros restring constantly, swing far faster than amateurs, and use setups tuned to their exact game; your best tension is the one that lets your own swing work.

Stringing, tension loss and when to cut strings out

Tension loss begins the moment a racket leaves the stringing machine. Strings stretch and settle, and the first twenty-four hours account for a noticeable share of total loss, which is why some serious players have rackets strung the day before a match rather than a week ahead. After that initial drop, tension declines more gradually with play, faster for soft strings and for heavy hitters. You cannot prevent this loss, but you can plan for it: if you know a string plays best at 54 pounds after settling, a stringer may pull slightly higher initially, and your own notes about how a fresh stringing feels versus a two-week-old one will teach you your personal pattern over time.

Knowing when to cut strings out is just as important as choosing a tension. Signs of dead strings include a dull, board-like feel, balls flying unpredictably, loss of spin, and increased arm discomfort without any change in technique. Polyester strings are notorious for going dead while still looking intact, which traps players into blaming their swing for a string problem. A simple test is to compare with a freshly strung racket of the same model: if the fresh one feels dramatically livelier and more predictable, the old strings were overdue. Cutting out dead strings feels wasteful, but playing with them costs more in lost enjoyment and potential arm strain.

Stringing itself deserves respect. A good stringer checks the frame, uses consistent technique, and records the string, tension, and date, often on a small label inside the throat. If you string your own rackets, calibrate the machine periodically and keep notes in the same format. Consistency from one stringing to the next is what makes tension experiments meaningful; without it, you cannot tell whether a change in feel came from the tension, the string, or the stringing. Treat your stringer as a partner in your game, communicate what you liked and disliked, and the setup will converge on something you trust.

Frequently asked questions

Does higher tension mean more power?

No. Lower tension generally gives more power and a softer feel, while higher tension gives more control and a firmer feel.

How often should I restring?

Roughly as many times a year as you play per week. Polyester strings go dead faster and may need more frequent restringing.

What tension should a beginner use?

Start at the lower end of the range printed on your racket frame, with a soft multifilament string, and adjust by 2 to 3 pounds from there.

Why does head size change the feel?

Larger heads have longer strings that deflect more, so the same tension feels softer. A slightly higher tension restores similar firmness.

What if my arm hurts?

Try a softer string, slightly lower tension, and check your grip size. Persistent pain deserves medical advice, not just a string change.

Should I copy a pro's tension?

No. Pros restring constantly and swing much faster. Your best tension is the one that lets your own swing work with control and comfort.
