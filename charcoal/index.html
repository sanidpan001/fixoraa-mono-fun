Why charcoal quantity is worth calculating

Every barbecue cook knows the sinking feeling of a fire that dies halfway through cooking, or the waste of a mountain of charcoal still glowing long after the food is done. Charcoal is not free, it takes time to light, and its heat is hard to adjust once it is burning, so getting the quantity roughly right matters more than most people think. Too little charcoal means cold spots, undercooked food and a second lighting that interrupts the meal. Too much means excessive heat that burns the outside before the inside cooks, plus wasted fuel and a longer wait for the coals to cool. Charcoal quantity depends mainly on the size of your grill and how long you plan to cook, with the type of cooking and the weather playing supporting roles. This calculator turns those two main factors into a practical weight and briquette count.

How the charcoal calculation works

The calculator starts from the grill's diameter, because a wider grill needs a wider bed of coals to cook evenly across its whole surface. The area of the grill grows with the square of its diameter, so a 57 centimetre kettle needs far more charcoal than a 47 centimetre one, even though the difference in diameter looks modest. That area figure is then scaled by cooking time: a quick half-hour grill for burgers needs only a thin bed, while a four-hour slow cook needs a deep bed plus spare fuel for topping up. The result is a weight in kilograms, converted into an approximate briquette count using a typical briquette weight of 45 grams. Lump charcoal burns hotter and faster than briquettes, so if you use lump, treat the result as a starting point and keep a little extra on hand.

Briquettes versus lump charcoal

Briquettes are uniform in size and burn time, which makes them the easiest choice for predictable cooking and for this calculator's estimates. They take a little longer to light and they produce more ash, but they hold a steady temperature for a long time, which is exactly what slow cooking needs. Lump charcoal, made from charred hardwood, lights faster, burns hotter and produces less ash, but the pieces vary in size, so the burn is less even and the total cooking time per load is shorter. For high-heat searing, lump is excellent; for a long, steady roast or smoke, briquettes are more reliable. Whichever you choose, never use self-lighting charcoal treated with lighter fluid for food, because it can leave a chemical taste, and never cook over charcoal that is still flaming: wait until the coals are covered with grey ash.

Step by step: using the calculator before you light up

Enter your grill diameter and your planned cooking time, including the time the food will actually be on the grill, not just the lighting time. The calculator returns a charcoal weight and a briquette count, plus a simple reminder to keep about a quarter extra for topping up on long cooks. Measure the charcoal with a chimney starter if you have one, because a full chimney is a repeatable measure and it lights the coals evenly without lighter fluid. Light the chimney, wait until the top coals are ashed over, and pour the coals out in the arrangement your cooking needs: spread evenly for direct grilling, or banked to one side for two-zone cooking with a hot side and a cooler side.

Direct, two-zone and slow cooking setups

Direct cooking, with coals spread under the whole grill, suits thin foods that cook quickly: burgers, sausages, steaks, vegetables and kebabs. Two-zone cooking, with coals on one side only, is far more versatile: sear over the hot side, then move food to the cool side to finish gently, which prevents burning and gives you a safe zone for flare-ups. For slow cooking and smoking, use a small, steady bed of coals with wood chunks for smoke, and top up with a few fresh briquettes every hour or so rather than dumping a large load at once. In all setups, control temperature with the vents, not by moving the food constantly: opening the bottom vent increases heat, closing it reduces heat, and the top vent should usually stay at least partly open so smoke can escape.

Weather, wind and charcoal safety

Wind steals heat from a barbecue, so on a windy day you will need more charcoal, a sheltered position, and a lid kept closed as much as possible. Cold weather has a similar effect, though a good kettle grill with its lid on loses surprisingly little heat. Rain is the hardest condition: keep the grill under cover if you can, but never barbecue inside a garage, tent or enclosed space, because charcoal produces carbon monoxide, which is colourless, odourless and deadly in enclosed air. Always barbecue outdoors in open air, keep children and pets away from the hot grill, and keep a bucket of water or sand nearby. When you finish, close all vents to starve the fire, and leave the ashes to cool completely, preferably overnight, before disposing of them.

Common charcoal mistakes

The most common mistake is lighting too much charcoal for a short cook, which creates fierce heat that is hard to tame and wastes fuel. The second is lighting too little for a long cook and then struggling to maintain temperature while guests wait. A third is cooking over flaming coals instead of waiting for the grey ash stage, which gives bitter, sooty flavours and burnt outsides. Another frequent error is using old, damp charcoal that has absorbed moisture in storage: keep charcoal dry in a sealed container or bag, because damp charcoal lights poorly and burns unevenly. Finally, do not pour lighter fluid onto already-lit coals, which is dangerous and unnecessary; a chimney starter and a couple of firelighters do the job safely every time.

Planning fuel for a party

For a party, calculate for the longest single cooking session, not for the total time guests will be present, because you can cook in batches over one well-managed fire. If you plan to grill for three hours in two batches, the calculator's three-hour figure is the right starting point, plus a little extra for topping up between batches. Keep a spare bag of charcoal unopened until you need it, and buy one size larger than you think for a first cook on a new grill, since every grill's appetite is slightly different. Note how much you actually used and how the temperature behaved, and next time your estimate will be personal and precise. Good barbecue cooking is mostly preparation: the right amount of fuel, lit properly, arranged thoughtfully, and managed with the vents, is what turns a stressful cook into a relaxed one.

Lighting and reusing charcoal

A chimney starter is the single best investment for charcoal cooking: fill it, light two firelighters underneath, and in fifteen to twenty minutes the coals will be ready. Partially burned charcoal from a previous cook can be reused: once it is completely cold, shake off the ash and mix it with fresh charcoal, because reused coals light faster but burn a little shorter. Never reuse charcoal that has been rained on or that smells of lighter fluid. Store fresh charcoal in a dry place, off the ground, and check the bag before a big cook so you are not caught short. With the quantity calculated, the fire lit properly and the food arranged by zone, you can focus on the cooking itself, which is, after all, the whole point.

Frequently asked questions

How much charcoal do I need for a 47 cm grill for 2 hours?

About 2.21 kg, or roughly 50 briquettes, plus a small spare amount for topping up.

Briquettes or lump charcoal: which is better?

Briquettes burn steadily and predictably, best for slow cooking. Lump burns hotter and faster, best for searing, but needs more frequent topping up.

When are the coals ready to cook on?

When they are covered with grey ash and no longer flaming. This usually takes 15 to 20 minutes in a chimney starter.

How do I keep a barbecue at a steady temperature?

Use the vents: open the bottom vent to increase heat, close it to reduce heat, and keep the lid closed as much as possible.

Can I reuse leftover charcoal?

Yes, once completely cold. Shake off the ash and mix reused coals with fresh charcoal, since they burn a little shorter.

Is it safe to barbecue in a garage?

No. Charcoal produces carbon monoxide, which is deadly in enclosed spaces. Always barbecue outdoors in open air.
