What a Roux Actually Is

A roux is flour cooked in fat — that is the entire ingredient list, and it is also one of the most powerful tools in cooking. The fat coats each starch granule so the granules disperse instead of clumping when liquid hits them, and the cooking toasts the flour, trading raw cereal flavor for nuttiness. Every great gravy, gumbo, étouffée, béchamel, and cheese sauce starts here. Master the ratio and the color stages, and thickened sauces stop being recipes you follow and become techniques you own.

The base proportion never changes: equal volumes of fat and flour. Two tablespoons of butter, two tablespoons of flour. What changes — the thing this calculator computes — is how much roux goes into how much liquid, and how dark you cook it.

The Thickness Ratios: 1-2-3 Per Cup

Memorize this and you can sauce anything: per cup of liquid, use 1 tablespoon each of fat and flour for a thin sauce (a light pan jus, a soup needing just a whisper of body), 2 tablespoons each for medium (classic gravy, béchamel for lasagna, pot-pie filling), and 3 tablespoons each for thick (hearty stew gravy, sausage gravy that stands on a biscuit, cheese sauce that coats). Four cups of medium gravy — the Thanksgiving standard — is 8 tablespoons of butter (one stick) plus 8 tablespoons of flour. That is the whole math; the calculator just scales it to your pot.

Thickness is also personal and contextual: gravy thickens as it cools, so aim a touch thinner than your target if the sauce will sit. Starches keep absorbing for several minutes off the heat — judge the final body after a 5-minute rest, not at the boil.

Fat Choices and Their Personalities

Butter is the default for light roux: unmatched flavor, and its milk solids brown beautifully in the first minutes. But those same milk solids burn over long cooking, so butter is wrong for dark roux. Neutral oil (or lard) is the gumbo cook's choice — high smoke point, no solids to scorch, clean canvas for 40 minutes of browning. Bacon fat makes a smoky Southern gravy that needs no other seasoning. Duck fat is the luxury move for upscale pan sauces. Clarified butter splits the difference: butter flavor with oil's heat tolerance.

Whatever the fat, use all-purpose flour. Bread flour's extra protein adds chew you do not want; specialty flours behave unpredictably. And measure flour by spooning it into the cup and leveling — packed flour can be 30% more, which is the difference between medium and paste.

The Color Stages: Flavor vs. Thickening

Here is the trade-off that defines roux cookery: the darker the roux, the deeper the flavor and the weaker the thickening. Cooking breaks down starch molecules, so a chocolate-dark gumbo roux has maybe half the thickening power of a white roux. The stages: white (2–3 min, for béchamel and cream sauces — cooked just past raw), blond (5–6 min, velouté and light gravies), peanut butter (10–15 min, the Cajun "peanut butter roux" for étouffée), copper/amber (20–25 min), brown (30–35 min), and dark chocolate (40+ min, gumbo).

Practical consequence: if you are making gumbo with a dark roux, use the thick ratio (or even a touch more) because the roux's thickening is diminished — the calculator's stage guide flags this. For a béchamel, white roux at the medium ratio is perfect, and the milk-white color stays pristine.

Technique: Low, Slow, and Constant

Melt the fat over medium heat, whisk in the flour until smooth, then settle in. Light roux needs a few minutes of easy stirring; dark roux demands 40+ minutes of constant stirring over medium-low with a flat-edged wooden spoon or heatproof spatula, scraping the corners where burning starts. Heavy-bottomed pots and cast iron are ideal — thin pans create hot spots that scorch one patch while the rest lags. If you must step away, pull the pot off the heat; roux goes from perfect to burnt in under a minute at the dark stages.

Judge by color and aroma, not the clock: dark roux should smell like toasted pecans and look like milk chocolate. Black specks or acrid bitterness mean it burned — there is no fix, no straining it out. Throw it away and start over; every Cajun grandmother has done exactly this.

Adding the Liquid Without Lumps

Lumps are a temperature-timing problem with two classic solutions. Method one: warm liquid into hot roux, gradually — add a ladleful, whisk smooth, repeat until thin enough to pour, then add the rest. Method two: cold liquid into hot roux all at once with vigorous whisking; the thermal shock actually helps dispersion. What fails is lukewarm liquid dribbled into lukewarm roux — that is paste city. Whichever method, bring the sauce to a gentle boil for 2–3 minutes after combining: starch needs boiling temperature to fully gelatinize and reach its final thickness, and the raw-flour taste cooks out.

Rescue moves: an immersion blender obliterates lumps in seconds; a splash more liquid thins an over-thick sauce; a slurry of extra roux (or a beurre manié — kneaded butter and flour) thickens an under-built one without lumps.

Roux vs. the Alternatives

Slurries (cornstarch or flour shaken with cold water) thicken fast and clear — great for stir-fry glazes and last-minute fixes — but they give a glossy, slightly slick body and no flavor, and they break under long simmering or freezing. Beurre manié (equal parts soft butter and flour kneaded together, whisked in bits at the end) enriches and thickens simultaneously, ideal for finishing. Reduction thickens by evaporation, concentrating flavor but taking time. Roux remains the champion when you want opaque, velvety body plus deep flavor built in from the start — which is why it anchors the mother sauces.

Make-Ahead and Storage

Roux keeps brilliantly: cool it, refrigerate up to a month, or freeze in ice-cube trays for instant portioned thickener (a dark roux cube dropped into weeknight soup is a weeknight upgrade). Dry-toasted flour roux (flour browned in a dry skillet, no fat) is the Cajun shortcut for gumbo — less rich, but shelf-stable and quick. Finished sauces with roux refrigerate 4–5 days and generally reheat well, though dairy-based ones prefer gentle reheating to avoid splitting.

Roux Around the World

Nearly every cuisine that makes gravy invented the roux independently. French mother sauces — béchamel, velouté, espagnole — are roux plus milk, stock, or brown stock, the foundation of classical Western saucing. Cajun and Creole cooking took the same technique to its darkest extreme: the chocolate roux of gumbo, cooked in oil for the better part of an hour, is arguably the single most important flavor in Louisiana cooking. Japanese curry roux blocks are a commercial shortcut version — toasted flour, fat, and curry spices pressed into cubes that melt into stew. Scandinavian brown gravies, Southern sawmill gravy with sausage drippings, and even the thickener in a proper Welsh rarebit all run on the same 1-2-3 ratios. Learn the technique once, and you have inherited a dozen food cultures' worth of sauces — the calculator's thickness ratios translate directly, whatever flag flies over the pot.

Putting It All Together

The workflow: choose your liquid quantity, set thickness by the 1-2-3 rule, pick a color stage for the flavor you want (and bump the ratio if you go dark), match the fat to the stage, and stir with patience. The calculator above turns those choices into spoonfuls and times — the rest is the meditative pleasure of watching flour and fat turn to liquid mahogany.

Frequently asked questions

What is the ratio of roux to liquid for gravy?

Per cup of liquid: 1 tablespoon each of fat and flour for thin gravy, 2 tablespoons each for medium, 3 tablespoons each for thick. So 4 cups of medium-bodied gravy needs 8 tablespoons (1 stick) of butter and 8 tablespoons of flour. This calculator scales it to any quantity.

What is the difference between white, blond, and brown roux?

It is cooking time and color: white roux cooks 2–3 minutes (for béchamel), blond 5–6 minutes (velouté), peanut-butter colored 10–15 minutes, copper 20–25, brown 30–35, and dark chocolate 40+ minutes (gumbo). Darker roux adds deep nutty flavor but thickens less, so gumbo recipes use more of it.

Should roux be equal parts butter and flour?

By volume, yes — the classic ratio is equal volumes of fat and flour (e.g., 2 tablespoons butter to 2 tablespoons flour). By weight it is also roughly equal since butter and flour have similar density per tablespoon. The ratio that actually varies is roux-to-liquid, which sets thin, medium, or thick.

Can I use oil instead of butter for roux?

Absolutely. Neutral oil is actually better for dark roux because butter's milk solids burn over the long cooking time. Butter gives the richest flavor for light roux (gravies, béchamel). Bacon fat adds smokiness for Southern gravy; duck fat is the luxurious choice for upscale sauces.

Why is my roux lumpy?

Lumps come from adding liquid too fast or at the wrong temperature. Fix: add warm (not cold) liquid gradually while whisking constantly, or add cold liquid to hot roux all at once and whisk vigorously — the temperature shock rule is one side hot, one side warm/cool, never lukewarm both. An immersion blender rescues a lumpy sauce in seconds.

How do you know when a dark roux is done?

Go by color and smell, not the clock: dark roux should look like milk chocolate and smell intensely nutty, like toasted pecans. It takes 40+ minutes of constant stirring over medium-low heat. If you see black specks or smell acrid bitterness, it burned — there is no saving burnt roux, start over.
