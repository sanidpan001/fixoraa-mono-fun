About this tool

Running out of wine at a party is a specific kind of social embarrassment, because it happens in front of everyone and it happens late, when shops are closed and nobody wants to leave. Buying far too much is the opposite problem: expensive, heavy to carry home, and wasteful if the bottles sit unopened for years. The sweet spot is a calculation, not a guess, and it depends on three numbers you can estimate before anyone arrives: how many guests are coming, what share of them will drink wine, and how long the party will last.

A standard wine bottle holds 750 millilitres, which pours five generous glasses of 150 millilitres each. Smaller pours stretch a bottle to six glasses, while generous host pours shrink it to four, so five is the honest planning figure. The average wine drinker at a social event drinks about three quarters of a glass per hour, once you average out the first-hour enthusiasm, the food breaks, and the guests who switch to water or soft drinks. Multiply guests by drinking share by hours by that pace, divide by five, and you have your bottle count.

The drinking share is the number most hosts overestimate. At a mixed adult party, roughly seventy percent of guests will drink wine at some point, while the rest prefer beer, cocktails, soft drinks, or nothing at all. At a wine-focused dinner the share can reach ninety percent, while at a family party with many children it can fall to forty percent of total guests. If you are unsure, seventy percent is a safe default, and it is better to count total guests and apply a share than to guess a drinker count from memory, because guest lists always contain surprises.

Party length matters more than people expect, because wine consumption is roughly linear with time. A two-hour drinks reception needs far less wine per person than a five-hour celebration dinner, even with the same guest list. Food slows drinking, so a dinner party needs slightly less wine per hour than a standing reception, while dancing and heat increase thirst and therefore consumption. WinePour uses a single steady pace for simplicity, but if your party has no food at all, consider rounding up by one or two bottles, and if it is a long seated dinner, the base figure will usually be exactly right.

The red-white split is where taste meets season. A common starting point is sixty percent red and forty percent white, which suits most mixed groups and most menus. In summer, or with seafood and salads, shift towards fifty-fifty or even more white and rosé, because chilled wines disappear faster in heat. In winter, or with roasts and stews, lean to seventy percent red, because guests reach for warming wines. Rosé deserves its own line in summer: count it as part of the white share or add a few bottles separately, and always keep it well chilled.

Sparkling wine plays a special role that the main calculation does not cover. If your party includes a toast, a birthday, or an arrival drink, budget one bottle of sparkling per six to eight guests for a single toast glass each, and keep it separate from the table wine count. Guests who drink sparkling all evening are rare, but if your crowd loves fizz, treat sparkling as part of the drinking share rather than as an extra. Champagne flutes hold less than wine glasses, so a sparkling bottle stretches to six or seven toast pours, which helps the budget.

Budget and quality follow a simple principle: one good wine beats two mediocre ones. Guests remember a pleasant bottle and forget a cheap one, but they also remember running out, so never cut the quantity to afford a fancier label. Buy from a retailer with a returns policy for unopened cases, because many wine merchants will take back unopened bottles, which turns overbuying from a loss into a refund. Keep a few bottles back in a cool place as a reserve, and open them only when the table bottles run low, so you always know exactly where you stand.

Serving details make the numbers work in practice. Chill white and rosé to around eight to ten degrees, and serve red slightly cool, around sixteen to eighteen degrees, rather than warm from a heated room. Open red wine an hour before serving if you can, and use proper glasses, because a good glass makes a modest wine taste better and slows over-pouring. Put water on the table as well, because hydrated guests drink more slowly, enjoy the evening more, and remember it better, which is the real point of the party.

Non-drinkers deserve the same care as drinkers, and they are part of the calculation, not an afterthought. The drinking share already accounts for them, but the table should still offer attractive alcohol-free options: good sparkling water, a quality alcohol-free wine or beer, and something interesting like a shrub or a mocktail. When non-drinkers feel included, the party feels generous, and the host who planned for everyone is the host people thank twice.

Wine planning is ultimately an act of hospitality. The maths takes thirty seconds, the shopping takes one trip, and the reward is a party where nobody counts bottles nervously and nobody leaves early to buy more. Enter your guests, your drinking share and your hours into WinePour, round the result with confidence, and spend the rest of your preparation on food, music and friends. A glass that is never empty is a small thing, but at a party, small things are what people remember.

One further consideration is glassware and pacing, because the vessel shapes the experience as surely as the wine inside it. Large-bowled glasses suit red wine, because they give the wine surface area to breathe and room to swirl, while smaller, narrower glasses preserve the chill and aromatics of white wine. You do not need a different glass for every wine at a party, but having two shapes, one for red and one for white, elevates the table noticeably. Pace the evening by opening bottles gradually rather than all at once, because an opened bottle oxidises over hours and a bottle opened late tastes fresher than one opened at six and finished at midnight. Keep opened red bottles away from radiators and direct heat, and return unfinished bottles to a cool place with the cork replaced, because even a day-old bottle can be perfectly pleasant if it was stored well.

A further practical matter is what to do with leftover wine, because a well-planned party often ends with two or three open bottles and the question of whether they will survive. Recorked red wine keeps for three to five days in a cool place, white wine keeps for five to seven days in the fridge, and sparkling wine keeps for only one to three days even with a proper stopper, so drink the fizz first. A simple vacuum pump stopper extends the life of still wine by a day or two by removing oxygen, while transferring leftover wine to a smaller bottle reduces the air in contact with it and preserves flavour noticeably better. Never pour leftover wine back into an unopened bottle, and label opened bottles with the date, because a wine that was delightful on Saturday can be flat and vinegary by Thursday, and the label saves you from an unpleasant surprise.

Frequently asked questions

How many glasses are in a bottle of wine?

A standard 750 ml bottle gives five glasses of 150 ml each, or six smaller pours.

How much wine does one person drink per hour?

On average about three quarters of a glass per hour at a social event, once food breaks and non-drinking periods are averaged in.

What share of guests will drink wine?

About 70 percent at a mixed adult party is a safe default; use 90 percent for wine-focused dinners and less for family parties.

How should I split red and white wine?

Start with 60 percent red and 40 percent white, shifting towards white and rosé in summer and towards red in winter.

How much sparkling wine do I need for a toast?

Allow one bottle per six to eight guests for a single toast glass each, kept separate from the table wine count.

Can I return unopened wine bottles?

Many wine retailers accept returns of unopened cases, so check the policy before buying and keep receipts.
