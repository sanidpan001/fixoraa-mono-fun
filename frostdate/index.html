What Are First and Last Frost Dates?

Every gardener eventually learns the same hard lesson: a single cold night can wipe out weeks of careful work. The last spring frost date is the average calendar date after which freezing temperatures are unlikely in spring, and the first autumn frost date is the average date when freezing temperatures return in fall. Together they define your growing season — the frost-free window in which tender plants can thrive outdoors.

These dates are not guarantees. They are 30-year climate averages published from official weather station records, and they carry a 50 percent probability: in roughly half of all years the last frost arrives earlier, and in half it arrives later. Think of them as the center of a bell curve, not a wall. Experienced gardeners treat the date as a guide, then add a safety margin of one to two weeks for their most tender crops like tomatoes, peppers, basil, and cucumbers.

How Frost Date Averages Are Calculated

Meteorologists compute frost dates from daily minimum-temperature records spanning the most recent 30-year climate normal period. For each year they note the last spring day that dipped to 32°F (0°C) or below and the first autumn day that did the same. Averaging those dates across 30 years gives the familiar single date you see on planting calendars.

Because a 50 percent probability still leaves real risk, many extension services also publish 90 percent dates — the date by which there is only a 10 percent chance frost will still occur. If you are planting expensive transplants or running a market garden, the 90 percent date is the safer number to use. A common rule of thumb: the 50 percent last-frost date plus 10 to 14 days approximates the 90 percent date in most of the continental United States.

One more nuance: official dates come from airport and regional weather stations, which are usually sited on flat, open ground. Your backyard may run several degrees warmer or colder, which is why observing your own microclimate (more on that below) matters as much as the published number.

Why Frost Dates Matter for Your Garden

Nearly every planting decision flows from these two dates. Warm-season crops — tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, squash, melons, basil, and beans — are damaged or killed by frost and should go out only after the last spring frost has passed, plus a safety margin. Cool-season crops — peas, spinach, lettuce, radishes, carrots, broccoli, and kale — tolerate light frost and can be sown weeks before the last frost date, which effectively stretches your season at both ends.

Seed packets speak this language too. When a packet says 'sow indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last frost,' it is telling you to count backward from your date. Get the date wrong by three weeks and your seedlings are either leggy and root-bound waiting indoors, or they go out into soil too cold to support them and stall for a month.

How to Use Your Frost Dates: The Count-Back Method

The count-back method is the single most useful scheduling trick in vegetable gardening. Start from your average last frost date and subtract the indoor lead time on the seed packet to find your sowing date. Here are realistic examples for a gardener whose last frost falls on April 20:

Frost vs. Freeze: Know the Difference

Not all cold nights are equal, and the distinction decides whether you need to panic or just throw a sheet over the peppers:

TermTemperatureWhat happens
Light frost32°F down to 29°FTender leaves show damage; hardy crops fine
Moderate frost28°F down to 25°FMost tender plants killed; semi-hardy damaged
Severe freeze24°F and belowNearly all unprotected growth destroyed

Frost dates technically mark 32°F, but a 'light frost' at 31°F on a calm night damages far less than 31°F with wind, which strips heat from leaves faster. Always read the forecast's expected low and the wind and cloud cover: clear, calm nights are the classic radiational frost setup.

Microclimates: Your Yard Is Not the Weather Station

Cold air behaves like water — it flows downhill and pools in low spots. A garden at the bottom of a slope can be 5°F colder than one on a gentle rise a hundred yards away. South-facing walls radiate stored daytime heat all night and can buy you a full hardiness zone of protection; the north side of a fence does the opposite. Urban gardeners often run a week ahead of official dates thanks to the heat-island effect, while rural gardeners in frost pockets run behind.

Spend one spring noting where frost lingers longest on your grass in the morning. Those white patches are your personal frost map, and they are worth more than any regional table for deciding exactly where to place tender crops.

Seven Ways to Protect Plants From a Late Frost

When a surprise freeze threatens after you have planted, you have options ranging from free to fancy:

  • Fabric row covers: spun-bonded polypropylene draped over hoops gives 2–6°F of protection and can stay on for weeks.
  • Cloches and buckets: an inverted bucket or milk jug over a single seedling is the fastest emergency shield; remove it in the morning so heat does not build.
  • Old bedsheets: never plastic directly on leaves — plastic conducts cold to the leaf surface. If you must use plastic, tent it on stakes.
  • Water walls: tubes of water around transplants absorb daytime heat and release it slowly overnight.
  • Deep watering: moist soil holds four times more heat than dry soil. Water the bed thoroughly before a frost night.
  • Mulch crowns: a thick straw mulch protects the crown of perennials even when top growth is sacrificed.
  • Christmas lights: old-style incandescent strings (not LEDs) under a cover add a few precious degrees on marginal nights.

Planning the Fall Garden With Your First Frost Date

The first autumn frost date runs the same count-back logic in reverse and unlocks a second season most beginners miss. Take your first frost date, subtract the crop's 'days to maturity' plus two weeks (growth slows as days shorten), and you have your last safe sowing date. With an October 25 first frost, carrots maturing in 70 days should be sown by early August; radishes at 25 days can go in as late as mid-September. Add a simple row cover and you can push harvests weeks past the first frost — kale and spinach often taste sweeter after a light freeze converts starches to sugars.

Bottom line: your two frost dates are the skeleton key to the whole gardening year. Look yours up above, count backward in spring and forward in fall, keep a cover handy for the surprises, and you will plant with the calm confidence that only comes from knowing your season.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly does 'last frost date' mean?

It is the average calendar date of the final spring day that reaches 32°F (0°C) or below, computed from 30 years of weather records. It carries about a 50% probability — in half of all years the last frost comes earlier, in half it comes later — so treat it as a guide and add 1–2 weeks of margin for tender crops.

How accurate are average frost dates?

They are reliable as long-term averages but any single year can deviate by two weeks or more. A late cold spring can push frost past the average, while an early warm spring brings it sooner. Always combine the average date with the 10-day forecast before transplanting tender crops.

What happens if I plant before the last frost date?

Cool-season crops (peas, spinach, radishes) will be fine, but warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers, basil, cucumbers) can be stunted or killed by even a light frost. If you plant early, be ready to cover plants with row cover, buckets, or sheets on any night forecast below 36°F.

What is the difference between a frost and a freeze?

Frost refers to temperatures at or just below 32°F where ice crystals form on surfaces; a light frost damages only tender growth. A freeze (28°F and below) means plant tissues themselves freeze, which kills most unprotected tender plants. Frost dates mark the 32°F threshold.

Why do frost dates vary so much from year to year?

Spring warming depends on large-scale weather patterns — jet stream position, Arctic air outbreaks, and ocean temperatures — which shift annually. The 30-year average smooths this out, but any given spring is a single draw from a wide distribution, so a 'late' frost year is completely normal.

Do frost dates matter in warm climates like Florida or Southern California?

Yes, but differently. Places like Miami average only a brief near-frost window, so the dates matter mainly for tropical plants and citrus protection. In these climates the bigger planning tools are heat, day length, and rainy seasons — though an outlier freeze every decade or so is exactly when the date proves its worth.
