Dashboard Warning Lights Guide

Tap the light. Know what to do.

Your Dashboard Speaks in Colour First

Modern cars can show dozens of dashboard symbols, and most drivers confidently recognize maybe six of them. The good news is you do not need to memorize every icon, because the colour tells you almost everything about urgency before you read a single pictogram. Every manufacturer follows the same broad code, set out in international standards:

  • Red — serious fault or safety risk. Stop as soon as it is safe. Do not keep driving.
  • Amber / orange — a fault is logged or a system is degraded. Investigate soon; drive gently meanwhile.
  • Green — a system is on and working normally. Information only.
  • Blue — usually just the main beam headlights. Dip them for oncoming traffic.
  • White / grey — system status. Nothing urgent.

Two extra rules sit on top of the colours. Flashing beats steady: a flashing light means the fault is happening right now and may be causing damage, while a steady one was logged at some point. And context matters: every warning light illuminates briefly when you turn the ignition on (a bulb check). One that never goes out failed its self-test; one that appears mid-journey means something just went wrong — treat the latter as more urgent.

The Red Zone: Stop Now Lights

Red means the car is shouting, not talking. The five red lights in this atlas are the ones that can end an engine or endanger lives if ignored.

Oil pressure (the little oil can) is arguably the most serious light on the dashboard. It does not mean the oil level is a bit low — it means oil pressure is gone, and without pressure the engine's moving parts are grinding metal on metal. A seized engine can follow within minutes. Stop immediately, switch off, and do not restart until the cause is found. Note: this is different from an oil-level warning (usually amber) — pressure is the killer.

Engine temperature (thermometer in waves) means the engine is overheating. Causes range from low coolant to a failed fan or a stuck thermostat. An overheated engine can warp the cylinder head — a repair that can exceed the car's value. Pull over, switch off, and never open the coolant cap while hot: the system is pressurized and can cause severe burns. Wait 30–45 minutes before checking the level.

Brake system (exclamation mark in a circle, sometimes the word BRAKE) can mean anything from the handbrake being left on to low brake fluid or a hydraulic fault. Check the handbrake first; if the light stays on, treat it as a brake fault and stop. Brakes are not optional.

Battery / charging (the battery symbol) means the alternator is not charging — the car is running on battery alone. You typically have 20–30 minutes of driving before the electrics die, and when they do the engine stops too. Turn off everything electrical (AC, heated screens, stereo) and drive straight to help.

Airbag / SRS means the airbag system has a fault and may not deploy in a crash. The car remains drivable, but its most important passive safety system is offline — get it diagnosed promptly, and know that a failed airbag self-test can also fail a roadworthiness inspection.

The Amber Zone: Service Soon Lights

Amber means the car is asking for attention, not demanding a stop — with one famous exception.

Check engine is the most-seen amber light and the vaguest: it covers everything from a loose fuel cap to a failing catalytic converter. The rule: steady check-engine means drive gently to a workshop and read the fault code; flashing check-engine means an active misfire that is dumping unburned fuel into the exhaust — stop driving now or you will destroy the catalytic converter. A $30 OBD2 reader (or a free code read at most parts stores) turns this light from a mystery into a specific fault code.

ABS means the anti-lock system has faulted. Normal friction braking still works fully — you can drive carefully to a workshop — but in an emergency stop the wheels can lock, so increase following distance and avoid hard braking until it is fixed.

TPMS (tyre pressure) means a tyre is significantly under-inflated (usually 25% below spec). Under-inflation overheats the tyre and can cause a blowout at speed, plus it wastes fuel. Check pressures — including the spare on older cars — and look for a slow puncture if one tyre keeps dropping.

Traction / stability control flashing briefly during a slide is normal — it means the system is working. If the light stays on, the system is disabled or faulted; drive cautiously in low grip and get it checked.

Fuel low is self-explanatory, but running the tank dry can overheat an in-tank fuel pump (it is cooled by the fuel) and, on diesels, introduce air into the system. Refuel within 30–50 km.

Glow plugs (diesels only, the little coil symbol) should go out after pre-heating. If it stays on or flashes, a plug or its control module is failing — expect hard cold starts until it is fixed.

Green and Blue: Information Only

Cruise control and high beam indicators simply confirm a system is active. The only action for blue high-beam is courtesy: dip your lights for oncoming traffic and when following another vehicle.

What To Do When Any Light Appears

  1. Read the colour. Red → plan to stop. Amber → plan a workshop visit. Green/blue → relax.
  2. Check steady vs flashing. Flashing upgrades any light by one urgency level.
  3. Check your manual. Icons vary slightly between manufacturers; the handbook is the authority for your exact car.
  4. Do not clear codes blindly. Erasing a check-engine code without fixing the cause just hides the symptom until the next drive cycle.
  5. After a battery change or jump-start, multiple lights at once is common and usually clears after a short drive as systems recalibrate. If they persist, investigate.

Why Lights Come On and Then Vanish

Intermittent lights are usually one of three things: a marginal sensor (an oxygen sensor on the edge of its range), temperature-sensitive wiring, or low system voltage during cranking. The fault code is still stored even after the light goes out — a code read will show it. Do not ignore a light just because it cleared itself; intermittent faults become permanent ones, usually at the worst moment.

The Bulb Check: Your Daily Self-Test

Every time you turn the ignition on, the dashboard lights up like a Christmas tree for a few seconds — that is the bulb check, and it is genuinely useful. Each warning light proving it can illuminate means the circuit behind it is alive. The habit to build: glance at the cluster during the bulb check and confirm the important ones appear. A warning light that never lights up — even during the bulb check — is a broken warning system, which is arguably worse than a fault it would have reported. Dead LEDs and tired bulbs hide real problems, and a seller who has 'fixed' a check-engine light by removing the bulb is committing fraud in most jurisdictions. If you are buying a used car and the check-engine light does not appear during the bulb check, walk away.

Diesel vs Petrol: Different Lights

A few lights only exist on one fuel type. Diesels have the glow-plug coil light for pre-heating, plus a DPF (diesel particulate filter) warning that means the filter is clogged with soot — usually cured by a sustained 15–20 minute run at motorway speed to trigger regeneration, not by a garage visit. Modern diesels may also show an AdBlue/DEF warning; ignore it long enough and the car will refuse to start once the tank runs dry. Petrol cars instead show misfire-related check-engine flashes more often (coil packs and spark plugs being wear items) and, on direct-injection engines, carbon-buildup symptoms that diesels rarely see. Knowing which lights your fuel type can show keeps you from Googling a diesel light on a petrol car.

EV and Hybrid Dashboard Lights

Electrified cars add their own symbols. A turtle/limp mode light means power is restricted — get to a charger or workshop promptly. Battery temperature warnings on EVs matter like coolant warnings on combustion cars: an overheating pack throttles charging and power. Hybrids show ready indicators (the car is 'on' but silent — a real hazard in garages and to pedestrians), and a charging system fault on an EV can mean the 12V auxiliary battery is dying even when the big traction battery is full. The colour code still applies: red means stop, amber means book service.

When the Manual Disagrees With the Internet

Dashboard icons are mostly standardized, but manufacturers add brand-specific symbols — BMW's myriad service icons, Tesla's touchscreen alerts, Toyota's hybrid system warnings — and the same pictogram can mean slightly different things between brands. When in doubt, your owner's manual wins over any website, including this one. Keep the manual in the glovebox (or the PDF on your phone) and check the exact symbol before acting on a generic guide's advice.

Frequently asked questions

What do the different dashboard light colours mean?

Red means stop as soon as it is safe — a serious fault or safety risk (oil pressure, overheating, brake fault). Amber means get it checked soon — a logged fault or degraded system. Green/blue/white are informational: a system is simply on.

Can I drive with the check engine light on?

A steady check-engine light means a fault is logged — you can usually drive gently to a workshop and read the code. A flashing check-engine light means an active misfire that can destroy the catalytic converter — stop driving immediately.

What is the most serious dashboard warning light?

Oil pressure is the most serious: it means the engine has lost oil pressure and internal damage is happening now. Stop immediately and switch off. Engine temperature (overheating) is a close second.

Why did several warning lights come on at once?

Yes. Multiple lights at once right after a jump-start or battery replacement is common — systems recalibrate after a short drive. If multiple lights appear while driving with no such cause, pull over: a major electrical fault may be developing.

My ABS light is on but the brakes feel fine — is that OK?

ABS faults disable only the anti-lock function; normal friction braking still works. Drive carefully to a workshop, keep extra following distance, and avoid situations needing emergency braking until it is repaired.

What does the tyre pressure (TPMS) light mean?

Usually a 25%-under-inflated tyre, sometimes a faulty sensor (sensors have batteries that die after 5–10 years). Check all pressures with a gauge first. If one tyre keeps dropping, you likely have a slow puncture.
