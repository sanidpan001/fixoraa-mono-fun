What CADR actually means

CADR — Clean Air Delivery Rate — is the single number that tells you how powerful an air purifier is. It measures the volume of filtered air a purifier delivers per minute, in cubic feet per minute (CFM), tested independently for smoke, dust and pollen. A purifier's CADR for smoke is usually the headline figure and the one to shop by. Marketing terms like "covers up to 1,000 sq ft" are unregulated; CADR is not. If two purifiers both claim a large room but one has a CADR of 120 and the other 300, the second cleans more than twice as fast.

The AHAM rule: CADR ≥ room area × 0.67

The Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers recommends a CADR at least two-thirds of your room's square footage. A 300 sq ft living room needs a CADR of at least 201; a 150 sq ft bedroom needs 101. This rule assumes standard 8 ft ceilings and roughly 4–5 air changes per hour (ACH) — the rate at which the purifier processes the room's entire air volume — which is the level studies link to meaningful reductions in particles, allergens and odours.

Air changes per hour (ACH), explained

ACH tells you how many times per hour a purifier filters the room's full air volume. For allergy and asthma relief, aim for 4–5 ACH; for general freshness, 2–3 ACH is fine. You can estimate ACH as (CADR × 60) ÷ room volume in cubic feet. The AHAM two-thirds rule bakes in roughly 4.8 ACH for 8 ft ceilings. High ceilings dilute the effect — a 12 ft ceiling needs about 50% more CADR than the basic rule suggests, so size up.

One big purifier or two smaller ones?

For large or L-shaped rooms, two smaller purifiers often beat one big one. Air purifiers clean best within about 10–15 feet; a single unit in a long room leaves dead zones at the far end. Two units also give redundancy — one can run on quiet low in the bedroom while the other works the living area — and replacement filters for small models are cheaper. The trade-off is two sets of filters to maintain and slightly higher total energy use. Compare on total CADR: two 150-CADR units (300 combined) outperform one 250-CADR unit in a 400 sq ft space.

HEPA, carbon and what to ignore

True HEPA (H13) captures 99.97% of 0.3-micron particles — the standard worth paying for. "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" are marketing terms with no guaranteed performance. An activated carbon filter handles odours, smoke and VOCs that HEPA cannot catch; it is essential if cooking smells or wildfire smoke are your concern. Ignore ionisers and UV lights as primary features — independent testing shows their contribution is minor next to a properly sized HEPA + carbon combination, and some ionisers produce ozone.

Placement and running tips

  • Give it breathing room: 1–2 feet of clearance on all sides, especially the intake.
  • Put it where the pollution is: near the kitchen for cooking particles, in the bedroom for allergies.
  • Run it continuously on low rather than blasting on high for an hour — steady filtration wins.
  • Close doors and windows in the room you are cleaning; you cannot filter the whole neighbourhood.
  • Replace filters on schedule: a clogged filter can halve real-world CADR. Most last 6–12 months.

How much should you spend?

Price roughly tracks CADR. Expect to pay for about $1 per CADR point in mid-range models — a 200-CADR purifier around $200. Budget models with honest CADR ratings exist, but beware no-name units that list room coverage without a CADR figure at all. Factor in filter costs: a cheap purifier with $80 replacement filters every 6 months quickly becomes the expensive option.

Size by CADR, buy True HEPA + carbon, run it low and steady — clean air is mostly arithmetic.

CADR vs "covers up to" claims

Manufacturers love room-coverage claims — "purifies up to 1,500 sq ft!" — but these are unregulated and usually assume one air change per hour, far below the 4–5 ACH that actually cleans air. A unit claiming 1,500 sq ft might have a CADR of 150, which by the AHAM rule properly serves about 225 sq ft. The rule of thumb: ignore coverage claims entirely and shop by CADR. If a listing hides its CADR, treat that as information — reputable brands publish it prominently because independent AHAM testing is a selling point.

Wildfire smoke: sizing up

During wildfire season, normal sizing rules go out the window. Smoke particles are fine (PM2.5) and infiltrate constantly through every gap, so target 5–6 ACH instead of 4–5 — roughly 25% more CADR than the AHAM minimum. Seal the room as best you can (tape, towels under doors), run the purifier on high before the smoke arrives to build a clean buffer, and prioritise the bedroom: eight hours of clean sleep air matters more than a pristine living room. Activated carbon becomes essential here — HEPA catches the particles, but only carbon adsorbs the gases and smell.

Noise, energy and running costs

A purifier running 24/7 on low is the effective strategy, so check the low-speed noise rating — under 30 dB is whisper-quiet; over 50 dB on high will annoy in a bedroom. Energy use is modest: most units draw 30–70 W, costing a few dollars a month continuously. The real running cost is filters: budget $50–100 per year per unit. When comparing models, divide the purchase price plus five years of filters by CADR — the "cost per clean air" reveals that some cheap units are the expensive choice.

Pre-filters and the maintenance schedule

The washable pre-filter is the unsung hero: it catches hair, dust and pet fluff before they reach the expensive HEPA filter, and vacuuming it monthly can double HEPA life. Mark your calendar: monthly — vacuum pre-filter, wipe the unit exterior; every 6 months — check carbon filter for odour breakthrough (if smells return, it is saturated); every 6–12 months — replace HEPA per the indicator or when airflow audibly drops. Running a purifier with clogged filters is like driving with the handbrake on — the motor works harder for a fraction of the CADR you paid for.

Purifiers and pets

Pet dander is exactly what HEPA filters excel at — 99.97% of 0.3-micron particles covers dander, dust-mite debris and most pollen. Size up one notch for pet homes since dander generates continuously, and place the unit where the pet spends time, not where you do. A carbon filter handles the litter-box and wet-dog odours HEPA cannot touch. One honest caveat: no purifier fixes an unwashed pet — regular grooming and washing bedding does as much as any machine. For severe allergies, the bedroom purifier plus keeping pets out of the bedroom beats any single device.

A worked example: sizing a two-bedroom flat

Take a 140 sq ft bedroom with 8 ft ceilings. AHAM minimum: 140 × 0.67 ≈ 94 CADR; buy 120+ for margin. Check ACH: (120 × 60) ÷ (140 × 8) ≈ 6.4 air changes per hour — excellent for allergies. For a 350 sq ft open-plan living area: minimum 235 CADR, so shop for 250+. Either one 250-CADR unit centrally placed, or two 130-CADR units at opposite ends for more even coverage. Total filter budget: roughly $100–150 per year across both rooms.

Five buying mistakes to avoid

  • Shopping by room-coverage claims instead of CADR — the number that actually predicts cleaning speed.
  • Buying "HEPA-type" instead of True HEPA (H13); only the latter guarantees 99.97% capture.
  • Undersizing the bedroom, where you spend eight hours breathing the same air.
  • Ignoring filter prices — a $99 purifier with $70 filters costs far more over five years.
  • Running it only when the air looks bad — particles you cannot see still need continuous filtration.

Frequently asked questions

What CADR do I need for my room size?

At least two-thirds of the room's square footage. A 300 sq ft room needs a CADR of 201 or higher. Round up to the next available model.

What does CADR mean on an air purifier?

CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) is the independently tested volume of clean air a purifier delivers per minute. It's the only reliable way to compare purifier power.

Are two small air purifiers better than one big one?

Often yes for large or L-shaped rooms — two units give more even coverage and let you run bedroom-quiet at night. Compare total CADR: two 150s beat one 250 in a big space.

What's the difference between HEPA and True HEPA?

True HEPA (H13) is certified to capture 99.97% of 0.3-micron particles. 'HEPA-type' or 'HEPA-like' have no guaranteed performance — always look for 'True HEPA'.

How often should I replace purifier filters?

Every 6–12 months depending on use and air quality. A clogged filter can halve real-world performance, so don't stretch it.

How do I calculate air changes per hour?

ACH = (CADR × 60) ÷ room volume in cubic feet. Aim for 4–5 ACH for allergies; the AHAM two-thirds rule already targets about 4.8 ACH at 8 ft ceilings.
