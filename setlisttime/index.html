    Why Setlists Always Run Long

    Every gigging musician knows the feeling: the setlist said 60 minutes, the clock says 78, and the venue manager is making throat-cutting gestures from the bar. Sets overrun for predictable reasons — tuning between songs, banter that felt like ten seconds but took two minutes, the singer's extended intro, the drummer's solo that nobody approved. The calculator above fights this with the only weapon that works: honest arithmetic. Songs times average length, plus breaks, plus encore. Twelve 4-minute songs with a 15-minute break is 63 minutes on stage. Write it down before the gig, not after.

    The formula is total = songs × average song length + break minutes, with encore songs added at the same average length. It looks almost insultingly simple, which is exactly why bands skip it — and exactly why they overrun. The discipline is not the math; it is measuring your actual average song length (most bands overestimate their tightness by 20%) and counting every pause.

    The Encore Question

    Encores are theatre, and theatre needs staging. If you plan an encore, it must be in the time budget — an unbudgeted two-song encore is 8–10 minutes of overrun. Decide in advance: is the encore real (leave the stage, wait for the chant, return) or is it the modern "we never leave, here's two more" style? Either way, the songs count. A good rule: never encore more than 15% of the set. Two songs after a twelve-song set is the sweet spot; four starts to feel like a second gig nobody asked for.

    Also plan the walk-off. The gap between set end and encore is dead air the audience fills with bar trips. Keep it under two minutes or the room's energy leaks out the door.

    Building an Energy Arc

    A setlist is a narrative, and energy is its plot. The classic arc: open hot (your second-best song — never the best first), dip slightly for a groove tune, build through the middle third to the peak (best song around the 70% mark), ease off for one breather, then close huge. The energy arc builder in the tool visualizes this — rate each song 1–5 and look at the shape. Warning signs:

    • Three slow songs in a row anywhere except a deliberate mid-set breather. The room will start checking phones.
    • Peak too early. If song 3 is the biggest moment, everything after feels like a comedown.
    • No dynamic contrast. Twelve songs at energy 4 is exhausting; the ear needs valleys to feel peaks.
    • New song buried at the end. Unfamiliar material works best mid-set when attention is highest, never as the closer.

    The Hidden Time: Tuning, Banter and Breakdowns

    Measure these once with a stopwatch at rehearsal and you will never guess again. Average per-song overhead for a typical four-piece: 30–60 seconds for tuning and instrument swaps, plus banter. That is 6–12 unbudgeted minutes in a twelve-song set — the entire overrun, explained. Solutions: a dedicated tuner and a second guitar on a stand, a setlist with keys and tempos grouped to minimize retuning, and a band agreement that banter gets one story per set, not five.

    Technical failures need a plan, not panic. Agree in advance: if a string breaks mid-song, does the band vamp or stop? (Vamp — always vamp. Stopping kills the room.) Keep spare everything within arm's reach of the stage, not in the van.

    Reading the Room

    The setlist is a plan, not a prison. Professional bands build in flex slots: two or three marked songs that can be swapped, skipped, or extended based on the crowd. Signs to stretch: dancing from song one, singing along, the bar queue empty because nobody wants to leave the floor. Signs to tighten: chatter rising over the music, phones out, the dreaded slow drift toward the exit. Have a designated "cut" song — ideally a mid-tempo one nobody will miss — that you can drop without breaking the arc.

    Never announce the flex. "We're going to skip one because you're boring" has never improved a gig.

    Festival vs Bar vs Theatre

    Bars want familiarity and volume discipline; the set can breathe, requests are fine, and a 10-minute overrun costs you nothing. Festivals want impact per minute; trim intros, stack the hits, end early rather than late. Theatres want dynamics and narrative; the audience is seated and listening, so the quiet song that dies in a bar becomes the night's highlight. One setlist does not fit all three — the calculator lets you re-time the same material for each room in seconds.

    Rehearsal Timing vs Stage Timing

    Rehearsal lies. Adrenaline speeds tempos up 5–10% on stage (songs get shorter), while crowd interaction stretches gaps (sets get longer). Net effect is usually +10–15% over rehearsal time. Rehearse the set as a performance at least once: no stopping, no "let's take that chorus again," full banter, timed with a phone on a music stand. The number you get is the only honest one.

    Printing and Sharing the Setlist

    Tape it to the stage floor — one copy per member, large font, with keys and tempos. ("Am — 128bpm" prevents the 45-second "what key is this in?" conference.) The copy-as-text button produces a clean list you can paste into the group chat, including the total time so everyone sees the budget. For the sound engineer, add the input list and any track cues; for the lighting person, mark the big moments. A setlist that only the band can read is a missed opportunity.

    Common Mistakes

    • Counting songs, not minutes. Twelve songs at 3 minutes and twelve at 5 minutes are different gigs.
    • Forgetting the changeover. Your slot includes getting on and off. A 60-minute slot with 10 minutes of changeover is a 50-minute set.
    • Soundcheck songs in the set math. Soundcheck is not the gig; do not let it eat set time.
    • No contingency. One spare song, rehearsed, for the "one more!" moment you actually want.
    • Ending on a slow song. The last thing the audience hears is what they remember. Close big, always.

    Do the math before the gig, build the arc, leave headroom for the chaos — and you will be the band that finishes exactly on time, which venues remember and rebook.

    Soundcheck and Changeover Math

    Your slot is not your set. A 60-minute slot with a 10-minute changeover is a 50-minute set, and bands that forget this start 10 minutes late and finish 10 minutes over — the cardinal sin of gigging. Work backwards from doors: changeover (get on, line-check, get off) typically eats 10–15 minutes for a four-piece with a shared backline, 20+ with full kit changes. Agree the changeover plan with the band before and after you before the gig, not in the corridor. If you are headlining, your changeover is part of the audience's evening — keep the house music up and the stage dark until you are genuinely ready, rather than noodling audibly for fifteen minutes.

    Soundcheck itself deserves its own arithmetic. A proper check runs 20–30 minutes for a full band: drums first (kick, snare, toms, overheads), then bass, guitars, keys, vocals last. The classic time-waster is the guitarist "just checking one more tone" while everyone stands around — set a rule that soundcheck is for the engineer, not for rehearsing. If doors are at 7 and the first band is at 8, the last soundcheck must end by 7:30, full stop.

    After the Gig: Reviewing the Set

    The bands that improve fastest debrief. Within 48 hours, while memory is fresh, ask three questions: where did the energy dip (check the arc you planned against what happened), what ran long (compare the calculator's number to reality), and what got the best reaction (that song moves earlier next time). Record every gig on a phone from the back of the room — audio is enough. You will hear what the audience heard, which is never what you heard on stage: the bass too loud, the vocals buried, the between-song dead air twice as long as it felt. Keep a gig log with date, venue, set length planned vs actual, and one line on the crowd. After ten gigs you will have a data-driven setlist instead of a hopeful one — and the calculator above makes re-timing it for the next room a two-minute job.

    Pacing the energy across the night

    Total minutes get you booked; pacing gets you rebooked. Map the set as a wave: open two songs hot to earn attention, dip into a mid-tempo groove while the room settles, then climb toward the peak three quarters in. Place the singalong where the crowd is loosest, never first. Talking between songs costs real time — budget thirty to forty five seconds per break and rehearse the transitions, not just the songs. If the venue cuts your slot by fifteen minutes mid-show, know in advance which two songs are droppable without breaking the arc, and keep the closer sacred.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many songs do I need for a 1-hour gig?

    About 12–14 songs at an average of 4 minutes each, with minimal breaks. Most bands should plan 12 songs plus one spare — the spare covers the 'one more!' moment without forcing an unplanned overrun.

    How do you calculate setlist duration?

    Multiply the number of songs by your true average song length, then add break minutes and encore songs. Example: 12 songs × 4 minutes + 15 minutes of breaks = 63 minutes. Measure your actual average at a full timed rehearsal — most bands underestimate by about 20%.

    Should I include the encore in my set time?

    Yes, always. An unbudgeted two-song encore adds 8–10 minutes of overrun. Decide in advance whether you'll do a real walk-off encore or stay on stage, and keep encores under about 15% of the total set.

    What is an energy arc in a setlist?

    The shape of intensity across your set: open hot, dip slightly, build to a peak around the 70% mark, breathe once, then close huge. Avoid three slow songs in a row and never put your biggest song third — everything after it will feel like a comedown.

    How much banter time should I plan?

    Budget 30–60 seconds of between-song overhead per song for tuning, swaps and talk — that's 6–12 hidden minutes in a 12-song set. Group songs by tuning, keep a second guitar on a stand, and agree on one story per set, not five.

    What should be on a printed setlist?

    Song titles in order, keys and tempos ('Am — 128bpm'), and marks for flex/cut songs and big lighting moments. Print one large-font copy per member and tape it to the stage floor — plus share the total timed duration so the whole band sees the budget.
