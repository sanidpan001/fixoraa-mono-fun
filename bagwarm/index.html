About BagWarm — sleeping bag temperature rating picker

Sleeping bag ratings promise a simple number, but behind that number is a testing standard, a human body, and a cold night, and understanding all three is what makes a good choice. Most modern bags are rated under the EN or ISO standard, which tests a bag with a heated manikin and reports three figures: comfort, limit and extreme. The comfort rating is the temperature at which a cold sleeper, typically a woman in the test, should sleep comfortably. The limit rating is the temperature at which a warm sleeper, typically a man, should sleep curled up without waking from cold. The extreme rating is a survival figure, not a comfort figure, and nobody should buy a bag based on it. BagWarm cuts through that confusion with a buffer: cold sleepers subtract five degrees from the expected low, average sleepers subtract three, and warm sleepers subtract none.

Your own sleep style is the biggest variable, and it is worth being honest about it. Cold sleepers feel cold in a house that others find comfortable, wear socks to bed, and wake chilled even in summer. Warm sleepers kick off covers, sleep with a window open, and rarely feel cold outdoors. Average sleepers sit between those extremes. Gender, body size, age, fitness and recent food and fatigue all shift the balance: a tired, hungry, smaller person loses heat faster than a rested, well-fed, larger person, and a damp bag loses insulation dramatically. That is why BagWarm's buffer is a starting point, not a guarantee, and why experienced campers carry a little extra warmth, in the form of a liner or an extra layer, rather than trusting a rating exactly.

The sleeping pad is half the system and is often the part people under-buy. A sleeping bag is compressed beneath your body, so its insulation there is almost useless, and the ground steals heat by conduction all night. Pads are rated by R-value, a measure of resistance to heat flow, and the right R-value depends on the ground and season. Summer camping on warm ground can work with an R-value of two, three-season camping wants three to four, and winter or snow camping wants five or more, sometimes by stacking two pads. A warm bag on a thin pad will still leave you cold from below, while a modest bag on a good pad can feel surprisingly warm, so budget for the pad as part of the sleep system, not as an afterthought.

Bag shape, fill and weight trade off against each other, and the right choice depends on how you camp. Mummy bags are narrow, light and warm because they minimise dead air space, but they feel restrictive to broad-shouldered or restless sleepers. Rectangular bags are roomy and comfortable for car camping but heavy and less efficient in cold weather. Down fill is light, compressible and long-lasting when kept dry, but it loses insulation when wet and costs more. Synthetic fill is bulkier and heavier but keeps much of its warmth when damp, dries faster, and suits wet climates and beginners. Consider pack size too: a bag that does not fit your pack, or forces you to leave other gear behind, is the wrong bag however warm it is.

Clothing and technique inside the bag matter as much as the bag itself. Wear dry base layers, a warm hat and clean dry socks, because a surprising amount of heat leaves through the head and feet, and damp daytime clothes will chill you as they dry. Eat a warm meal before bed and keep a snack and water nearby, because your body generates heat by burning fuel, and a hungry body is a cold body. Do not wear a heavy jacket inside a tight bag: compressed insulation insulates poorly, and a bag that is too tight around the shoulders will feel colder than a roomier one. A silk or fleece liner adds a few degrees, keeps the bag cleaner, and is easier to wash than the bag itself.

Weather and campsite choice change the temperature you actually experience, and the forecast low is only part of the story. Wind strips heat from a tent, humidity makes cold feel deeper, and a valley floor can be several degrees colder than a hillside because cold air pools downhill. A tent protects from wind and dew but adds only a degree or two of warmth on its own, and a wet tent or a bag that touches a condensation-wet tent wall will lose insulation. Pitch on ground that is dry and slightly raised, shelter from wind with natural features where possible, and ventilate the tent enough to reduce condensation without creating a draught. If the forecast is uncertain, pack for the colder end of the range, because being too warm can be fixed by unzipping, while being too cold is miserable and potentially dangerous.

Children, beginners and anyone camping in genuinely cold conditions need extra caution. Children lose heat faster relative to their size and may not wake or complain clearly when cold, so give them a warmer-rated bag, a good pad and dry layers, and check on them during the night. Beginners often underestimate night temperatures, especially in mountains and deserts where days are warm and nights plunge, so BagWarm's buffer is particularly valuable for a first trip. In sub-zero conditions, treat the bag rating as a minimum, carry a vapour barrier or extra insulation if you are experienced, and know the signs of hypothermia: shivering, confusion, slurred speech and clumsiness are warnings to warm up immediately, not to tough out until morning.

Worked examples show the buffer in action. A cold sleeper facing a zero-degree night should choose a bag rated at minus five degrees or warmer, because five degrees of buffer are subtracted from zero. A warm sleeper facing a five-degree night can choose a five-degree bag, with no buffer subtracted. An average sleeper facing a minus-five-degree night should choose a bag rated at minus eight degrees or warmer, because three degrees are subtracted from minus five. Enter your own expected low and sleep style, check your pad's R-value, pack dry layers, and you will sleep through nights that catch less prepared campers shivering.

Sleeping bag liners, quilts and bivvy bags extend a sleep system in ways that a single bag rating cannot capture, and they are worth understanding before buying a warmer, heavier bag. A silk liner adds about two to three degrees of warmth, weighs very little, and keeps the bag clean, while a fleece liner adds about five degrees but is bulkier and heavier, making it a better choice for car camping than for backpacking. A quilt, which has no hood and no insulation underneath, pairs with a good pad and suits warm sleepers and summer camping, because it is lighter and roomier than a full bag, though it needs practice to avoid draughts at the neck and sides. A bivvy bag adds wind and splash protection outside the bag, useful for emergency shelter and for sleeping under a tarp, but it traps condensation if not ventilated, so it is not a simple warmth upgrade. Layering two bags, a summer bag inside a three-season bag, can work for winter, but the inner bag must not be so compressed that its insulation fails, and the combination is often heavier than a single winter bag. For couples, double bags and quilt systems suit car camping and warm weather, but they share warmth unevenly if one partner sleeps colder, so separate bags with a shared blanket are often more comfortable. Whatever system you choose, test it in the garden or on a short trip before a remote expedition, because a cold night twenty miles from help is a poor time to discover that your liner slips, your quilt draughts, or your pad deflates slowly through the night.

Frequently asked questions

What sleeping bag rating do I need?

Subtract a buffer from your expected overnight low: 5 °C for cold sleepers, 3 °C for average sleepers, 0 °C for warm sleepers. Buy a bag rated at that temperature or lower.

What is the difference between comfort, limit and extreme ratings?

Comfort suits cold sleepers, limit suits warm sleepers, and extreme is a survival figure only — never buy a bag based on the extreme rating.

Does the sleeping pad matter?

Yes, enormously. Your body compresses the bag beneath you, so the pad insulates you from the ground. Use R-4+ for cold nights and R-2 to R-3 for summer.

Is down or synthetic better?

Down is lighter and more compressible but loses warmth when wet. Synthetic is bulkier but keeps warmth when damp and suits wet climates.

Why am I cold in a bag rated for the temperature?

Possible causes: a thin pad, damp clothing or bag, wind, hunger, fatigue, or a bag that is too tight. Add a liner and dry layers.

Should I wear a jacket inside my sleeping bag?

A light dry base layer is best. A heavy jacket compressed inside a tight bag insulates poorly and can make you colder.
