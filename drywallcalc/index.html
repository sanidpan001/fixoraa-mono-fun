    How much drywall do you actually need?

    Drywall (plasterboard) estimating trips up DIYers because the material comes in fixed sheet sizes while rooms come in awkward dimensions. The reliable method: measure the total wall and ceiling area, subtract doors and windows, add 10% for cutting waste, then divide by the usable area of one sheet. A standard 4×8 ft sheet covers 32 sq ft, or about 2.98 m². Getting this right matters — drywall is cheap per sheet but bulky to transport, and a second delivery wipes out your savings.

    Measuring walls and ceilings

    For each wall, multiply width by height. A 12 ft × 8 ft wall is 96 sq ft. Do this for all four walls, then add the ceiling (length × width). Now subtract openings: a standard interior door is about 21 sq ft (3×7 ft), a typical window about 12–15 sq ft. Do not subtract tiny openings like electrical boxes. If your ceiling is higher than 8 ft, note that standard sheets are 8 ft long — a 9 ft wall needs either 9 ft sheets (available at most suppliers) or a horizontal strip, which adds waste.

    How many screws per sheet?

    Plan on 32 screws per 4×8 sheet: screws every 12 inches along studs in the field and every 8 inches along edges. For a full room this adds up fast — a 20-sheet job needs 640 screws, so buy the 5 lb box rather than small packs. Use 1¼-inch coarse-thread screws for ½-inch drywall on wood studs, and fine-thread screws for metal studs. Drive heads just below the paper surface without tearing it; a dimpler bit or screw gun with depth control pays for itself on the first wall.

    Screws vs nails

    Screws beat nails for DIY: they hold better, rarely pop, and are easy to back out if you misplace one. Ring-shank nails are acceptable for professionals with nail guns, but screws are the safer choice for everyone else.

    Joint compound: how much to buy

    Budget roughly 1.5 kg of joint compound per sheet for a standard three-coat finish. That covers taping, two fill coats and the final skim. All-purpose compound works for every coat, though pros often use quick-setting compound for the first coat and lightweight topping compound for the finish. A 20-sheet room needs about 30 kg — typically two large buckets or one bucket plus a bag of setting compound. Do not skimp: thin coats that need re-doing cost more time than generous first coats.

    Tape, corner bead and extras

    • Joint tape: one 250 ft roll covers roughly 8–10 sheets. Paper tape is stronger than mesh; mesh is easier for beginners.
    • Corner bead: count your outside corners and buy one 8 ft bead per corner, plus a few spares.
    • Primer: always prime new drywall before painting — unprimed drywall drinks paint unevenly and flashes.
    • Moisture areas: use moisture-resistant (green) board in bathrooms, never standard board behind tile in showers (use cement board there).

    Hanging tips that save hours

    Hang ceilings first, then walls — wall sheets support the ceiling edges. Keep a ½-inch gap at the floor for expansion and to keep the board off damp concrete. Stagger vertical joints between rows like brickwork so no four corners meet. Mark stud centres on the floor and ceiling before hanging so you always know where to drive screws. And lift with your legs or rent a drywall lift for ceilings: a 4×8 sheet of ½-inch board weighs about 26 kg.

    Drywall thickness: which to buy

    ThicknessUse
    ¼ inch (6 mm)Curved surfaces, overlaying old walls
    ⅜ inch (10 mm)Renovation overlays, patching
    ½ inch (12 mm)Standard walls and ceilings — the default
    ⅝ inch (16 mm) Type XFire-rated assemblies, garages, ceilings with 24-inch joist spacing

    ½-inch board suits almost every residential job. Step up to ⅝-inch Type X where building code demands fire resistance (between garage and house, furnace rooms) — it is heavier and harder to cut, so reserve it for where it is required.

    Cutting and hanging technique

    Score the paper face with a sharp utility knife, snap the board over a straight edge, then cut the back paper — clean cuts in seconds, no sawing. Cutouts for outlets: measure, mark, and use a jab saw or rotary tool. Hang sheets tight but not forced; ⅛-inch gaps at joints are fine and get filled with compound. Drive screws every 12 inches in the field and 8 inches on edges, dimpling heads just below the surface. A second pair of hands (or a rented lift for ceilings) turns a miserable job into a manageable one.

    What does drywalling cost?

    Materials are cheap: a 4×8 sheet of ½-inch board is a few dollars/pounds, so a 20-sheet room's materials (board, screws, tape, compound, bead) often total under $300. Labour is the real cost — professional hanging and finishing runs several dollars per square foot, which is why DIY drywall is one of the highest-payback skills a homeowner can learn. Finishing (taping and mudding) is the skill that takes practice; hanging is just measuring and lifting.

    Working around obstacles

    Real rooms have pipes, outlets and odd angles. For electrical boxes, measure from a fixed reference (floor and adjacent stud), transfer to the sheet, and cut with a jab saw — cut slightly small and enlarge; you cannot shrink a hole. Pipes get a U-shaped notch cut from the sheet edge, then the offcut is fitted back around the pipe. For angled bulkheads and sloped ceilings, make cardboard templates first — drywall is cheap but your time is not. Keep offcuts larger than a foot; they patch the inevitable mistakes.

    Soundproofing upgrades worth doing

    If you are opening walls anyway, consider acoustic upgrades while access is easy: resilient channel decouples drywall from studs and dramatically cuts sound transfer; a second layer of ⅝-inch board with acoustic sealant between layers adds mass cheaply; and mineral-wool insulation in the cavity absorbs mid-range frequencies. None of these change the sheet count much (double-layer jobs simply double it), but they must be planned before the first sheet goes up. For home theatres, bedrooms and party walls, the extra material cost pays back every quiet night.

    Estimating for whole-house jobs

    For multi-room projects, calculate each room separately rather than lumping areas together — waste does not average out across rooms with different shapes, and separate lists prevent one room's offcuts from being double-counted. Order 5% extra beyond the per-room 10% for whole-house work to cover damaged sheets in delivery. Schedule delivery after framing inspection passes but before flooring goes in; drywall dust and floor finishes are enemies. Stack sheets flat, off the floor, in the rooms where they will hang.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many drywall sheets do I need per square foot?

    A standard 4×8 ft sheet covers 32 sq ft (2.98 m²). Divide your net area (minus doors and windows) by 32 and add 10% for cutting waste, then round up.

    How many screws per sheet of drywall?

    Plan 32 screws per 4×8 sheet — roughly one 5 lb box per 20 sheets. Use 1¼-inch coarse-thread screws for wood studs.

    How much joint compound do I need?

    About 1.5 kg per sheet for a standard three-coat finish. A 20-sheet room needs roughly 30 kg of joint compound.

    Should I add waste allowance to drywall estimates?

    Yes — add 10% for cutting waste on straightforward rooms, more for rooms with many angles, closets or bulkheads where offcuts pile up.

    Can I use regular drywall in a bathroom?

    No. Use moisture-resistant (green) board in bathrooms and cement backer board behind tile in showers. Standard drywall fails quickly in wet areas.

    What thickness of drywall should I use?

    ½-inch for standard walls and ceilings, ⅝-inch Type X where fire rating is required (garages, furnace rooms), ¼-inch for curves or overlays.
