About HerbDry — herb drying calculator

Fresh herbs are mostly water, and drying is simply the controlled removal of that water until microbes can no longer grow and flavour compounds are concentrated rather than lost. A typical leafy herb is about three quarters water, so one hundred grams of fresh basil becomes roughly twenty-five grams of dried leaves, while woody rosemary, with thicker, oil-rich leaves, keeps closer to thirty grams. Those ratios are the heart of HerbDry: the calculator does not guess a single number for every herb, because basil, mint, parsley and rosemary genuinely dry down differently. Knowing the expected yield before you start also stops a common disappointment, which is drying a huge-looking bunch and ending with a teaspoon of leaves, or the opposite problem of overloading a dehydrator tray.

Air-drying is the oldest method and still one of the best for flavour, provided the air is warm, dry and moving. Tie small bunches loosely, hang them upside down in a shaded, ventilated spot, and expect seven to fourteen days depending on humidity and leaf thickness. Direct sunlight bleaches colour and drives off volatile oils, so shade matters more than heat. A dehydrator gives the most consistent result, usually at 35 to 45 degrees Celsius, with thin leaves done in four to eight hours and thicker herbs taking longer. An oven on its lowest setting with the door slightly ajar works in a hurry, but it is the easiest method to overdo, and overheated herbs taste dusty rather than fragrant, so check every thirty minutes.

Preparation makes more difference than most beginners expect. Harvest in the morning after dew has dried, when essential oils are at their peak, and discard any yellowed or damaged leaves. Wash only if needed, then pat thoroughly dry, because surface water slows drying and invites mould. Strip leaves from thick stems for leafy herbs, but keep woody herbs on short sprigs until dry, when the leaves strip off easily. Spread leaves in a single layer with space between them, never in a deep pile, because trapped moisture in the centre of a pile is where mould starts. Turn or stir once a day for air-dried herbs, and rotate dehydrator trays so every tray dries evenly.

The test for doneness is touch, not time. Leaves should crumble easily between your fingers and stems should snap cleanly rather than bend. If a leaf still feels leathery or cool and pliable, it still holds water, and sealing it in a jar will trap that moisture and spoil the batch. When in doubt, give herbs another day or another hour, and then condition them: put the dried herbs in a jar, shake daily for a week, and watch for condensation on the glass. Condensation means the herbs need more drying. Properly dried herbs feel papery, smell strong when crushed, and keep their colour, while under-dried herbs smell faintly musty and clump together in storage.

Storage is where good drying pays off or is wasted. Keep dried herbs whole, not ground, in airtight glass jars away from light, heat and steam, because grinding exposes far more surface to air and flavour fades in weeks rather than months. A kitchen cupboard away from the stove is ideal, while a sunny windowsill or a jar above the kettle is not. Label every jar with the herb and the drying date, and use home-dried herbs within six to twelve months, checking aroma before use: crush a pinch, and if the scent is weak, use more or replace the jar. Whole dried leaves also make it easy to spot a poor batch, since faded colour and weak scent show up immediately.

Different herbs reward different methods, and learning those preferences saves flavour. Delicate basil loses aroma quickly in heat, so gentle air-drying or a low dehydrator setting suits it best, and many cooks prefer freezing basil for sauces instead. Mint and oregano air-dry beautifully and keep a strong scent. Rosemary, thyme and bay are sturdy, dry reliably by almost any method, and store well for a year. Parsley is the trickiest common herb, because it dries slowly and can taste flat if overheated, so patience and low heat matter. Chives lose much of their character when dried, which is why freezing is often the better choice for them, and HerbDry users should treat its yield for chives as a rough guide only.

Yields also help with cooking, because dried herbs are far stronger than fresh ones by weight and volume. The classic substitution is one teaspoon of dried herbs for one tablespoon of fresh, roughly a one-to-three ratio, but that rule varies with the herb and with how finely it is crumbled. Start with less than you think you need, especially with potent herbs like oregano, rosemary and sage, and taste before adding more. Dried herbs need time and moisture to release flavour, so add them early in cooking, unlike fresh herbs, which are often stirred in at the end. For dressings and cold dishes, soak dried herbs in a little liquid or oil for ten minutes first.

Worked examples make the calculator concrete. One hundred grams of fresh basil at a factor of 0.25 gives twenty-five grams dried, enough to fill a small jar. Two hundred grams of fresh mint at 0.22 gives forty-four grams dried, a generous winter supply for tea and cooking. Fifty grams of fresh rosemary at 0.30 gives fifteen grams dried, which sounds small but seasons many meals because rosemary is so potent. Weigh your harvest, choose the closest herb factor, pick a method, and let the calculator set expectations before you tie a single bunch.

Once herbs are dry, grinding, blending and using them well is a second skill that repays a little care, because dried herbs behave differently from fresh ones in every recipe. Grind only what you need for a week or two, using a dedicated spice grinder or a mortar, and keep the rest whole, because whole leaves protect flavour far longer than powder. A simple herb blend for winter cooking can be made from dried oregano, thyme, rosemary and marjoram in equal parts, crumbled together and stored in a small jar, while a tea blend might combine mint, lemon balm and chamomile, steeped for five minutes in just-boiled water. Dried herbs need time to rehydrate, so add them to soups, stews and sauces at least twenty minutes before serving, and crush them between your fingers as you add them, which releases oils and breaks the leaves into a usable size. For salads and cold dishes, soak dried herbs in vinegar, lemon juice or oil for ten minutes first, or they will taste dusty and remain visibly dry on the leaf. Parsley and mint dried at home are excellent in grain salads, tabbouleh-style dishes and herb butters, where their concentrated flavour stands up to lemon and garlic. Basil dried at home is best in cooked tomato sauces, soups and baked dishes, rather than in raw pesto, where fresh basil is irreplaceable. Label blends with ingredients and dates, taste before using, and replace any jar that smells faint or musty, because a weak herb can be used in larger quantity, but a stale herb adds nothing but colour. Drying your own herbs also changes how you garden, because you begin to harvest regularly rather than letting plants bolt, and regular cutting encourages bushy growth, more leaves, and a longer season, so the drying rack and the garden feed each other all summer.

Frequently asked questions

How much dried herb do I get from fresh herbs?

Leafy herbs dry to roughly 20–25% of their fresh weight; woody herbs like rosemary keep about 30%. Enter your fresh weight and herb in the calculator for an estimate.

How do I know herbs are fully dry?

Leaves should crumble easily and stems should snap, not bend. If leaves feel leathery, dry them longer before jarring.

What is the best drying method?

Air-drying in shade with airflow preserves flavour well; a dehydrator at 35–45 °C is fastest and most consistent; a low oven works but needs frequent checking.

How long do dried herbs last?

Kept whole in airtight jars away from light and heat, most home-dried herbs stay good for 6–12 months.

Why did my dried herbs go mouldy?

They were probably jarred while still holding moisture, or dried in a pile with poor airflow. Dry in a single layer and condition jars for a week.

Can I dry herbs in sunlight?

Shade is better. Direct sun bleaches colour and drives off the volatile oils that carry flavour.
