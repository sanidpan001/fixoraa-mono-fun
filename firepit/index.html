Why fire pit size is a seating question first

Most people choose a fire pit by how it looks in a catalogue, and only discover afterwards that it is too small for the chairs around it, too large for the patio, or too close to the fence. A fire pit is really a social space: its diameter decides how many people can sit comfortably around it, how far they sit from the heat, and how much open ground it needs for safety. A pit that is too small forces guests to crowd in or sit too far back to feel the warmth. A pit that is too large dominates a small garden and radiates more heat than anyone wants on a mild evening. FirePit starts from the diameter and works out the seating, the footprint and the clearance, so the pit you buy fits both your garden and your gatherings.

How the seating calculation works

The calculator uses the circumference of the fire pit, which is pi multiplied by the diameter, because seating goes around the edge. Allowing about 60 centimetres of rim per person gives a comfortable elbow room for an adult in an outdoor chair, without crowding and without leaving guests perched on the edge of the group. Dividing the circumference by 60 and rounding down gives a realistic seat count, not an optimistic one. The calculator also gives the footprint area, so you can check the pit against your patio, and it reminds you of the clearance rule: keep the pit well away from fences, trees, overhanging branches and buildings, and never place it under a covered structure unless that structure is specifically designed for fire.

Choosing the right diameter for your space

A diameter of about 90 centimetres suits a small patio and four people, and it is easy to fuel with a modest amount of wood. A diameter of 120 centimetres is the versatile middle ground: it seats about six people, it throws enough heat for a cool evening, and it fits most medium gardens. A diameter of 150 centimetres seats about seven or eight people and suits larger gatherings, but it needs a large, level area and considerably more fuel. Above 150 centimetres, a fire pit becomes a statement piece that needs a generous garden and careful thought about smoke drift to neighbours. Whatever size you choose, measure your patio first: allow at least a metre of clear, level ground around the pit for chairs and safe movement, plus the clearance to anything flammable.

Step by step: planning with FirePit

Enter the diameter you are considering, or the diameter of a pit you already own, and the calculator returns the seat count, the circumference and the footprint. Then lay it out in the garden: mark the circle with a rope or a hose, place the chairs you actually own around it at a comfortable distance, and sit in them. That physical test is worth more than any number, because chair sizes vary and people sit at different distances from the fire depending on the weather. Check the view from the house, the path guests will take with drinks in the dark, and the direction smoke usually drifts. A pit that looks perfect on paper but sends smoke into a neighbour's bedroom window every evening will not be used for long.

Wood, gas and smokeless fire pits compared

Wood-burning pits give the classic crackle, smell and dancing flame, and they are the cheapest to run if you have a supply of dry hardwood. They also produce the most smoke and ash, and they need the most clearance. Gas fire pits light instantly, produce no ash and can be turned down or off at once, which suits small patios and neighbours, but they need a gas supply and they give a less dramatic flame. Smokeless wood pits, with a double-wall design that burns off smoke, are a genuine improvement for many gardens: they burn hotter and cleaner, though they still need dry wood and proper clearance. Whichever type you choose, use only dry, seasoned wood in a wood pit, never treated timber, painted wood or household rubbish, which produce toxic smoke and damage the pit.

Safety, clearance and the law

Fire safety is not optional, and it starts with position: keep the pit at least three metres from buildings, fences, trees and anything flammable, and much further if local rules require it. Some areas restrict open fires, especially during dry periods or under burn bans, so check local regulations before you buy or build. Never leave a fire unattended, keep children and pets at a safe distance, and keep a bucket of water, sand or a fire extinguisher nearby. Let the fire burn down naturally and leave the ashes to cool completely, preferably overnight, before disposing of them, because buried embers can stay hot for many hours. A fire pit used safely is a joy; a fire pit used carelessly is a hazard to your home and your neighbours.

Seating, comfort and layout tips

Low, wide chairs suit a fire pit better than tall dining chairs, because they put you at the right height to enjoy the warmth without leaning over the flames. A ring of chairs with small side tables for drinks works better than a bench, because people can move closer or further from the heat as the evening changes. Leave a gap in the ring for the person tending the fire, and keep a clear path for carrying wood. Lighting around the seating area, not just the fire itself, prevents trips in the dark, and a few blankets for cool evenings extend the season considerably. If your garden is small, consider a fire bowl on a table rather than a ground pit: it gives the same social focus in a smaller footprint, though the seating calculation still applies.

Fuel, maintenance and making the fire last

Dry hardwood, such as oak, ash or birch, burns longest and cleanest; softwood burns fast and sparks more, so it is better for kindling than for the main fire. Start with kindling and small sticks, add larger logs gradually, and resist the urge to overload the pit, which smothers the fire and produces smoke. A well-built fire needs airflow, so do not pack logs tightly. Clean ash out regularly, because a deep ash bed chokes airflow, and check metal pits for rust and stone pits for cracks each season. Cover the pit when not in use to keep rain out, since a pit full of water is unpleasant to light and can crack in frost. With the size planned, the seating tested and the safety rules respected, a fire pit becomes the natural centre of summer evenings.

Designing the area around the pit

The area around a fire pit deserves as much thought as the pit itself. A level base of gravel, stone or pavers keeps chairs stable and prevents the ground from scorching, while grass directly under a pit will die and become muddy. A low stone wall around the seating area can double as extra seating and as a windbreak, which makes the fire burn better and the guests more comfortable. Planting around the area should be low and away from the heat, not overhanging, and any wooden decking must be protected with a proper fireproof base, or the pit should be placed elsewhere entirely. Plan the whole circle, pit plus chairs plus clear ground, and the result will feel intentional, safe and inviting for years.

Fire pits through the seasons

A fire pit earns its place not just on summer evenings but through much of the year, provided the seating and shelter suit the season. In spring, a fire pit extends the evening just enough to enjoy the garden after sunset, and a windbreak of planting or a low wall makes a real difference on cool nights. In autumn, dry leaves must be cleared from around the pit before lighting, because wind-blown leaves are a fire risk, and damp logs should be avoided since they smoke heavily. In winter, a fire pit can still be wonderful on a clear, still evening, but guests will sit closer and the fire will need more fuel, so plan for a larger wood supply and warmer seating. Whatever the season, the same rules apply: dry wood, safe clearance, a fire that is never left alone, and ashes left to cool completely. A pit planned with its seating and its safety in mind becomes a year-round feature, not just a summer novelty.

Frequently asked questions

How many people fit around a 120 cm fire pit?

About 6 people, allowing 60 cm of rim per person. A 90 cm pit seats about 4, and a 150 cm pit about 7.

How far should a fire pit be from a house?

At least 3 metres from buildings, fences, trees and anything flammable, and further if local rules require it.

Wood, gas or smokeless: which fire pit is best?

Wood gives the classic flame and smell but most smoke. Gas is instant and clean. Smokeless wood pits burn hotter and cleaner than open wood pits.

What wood should I burn in a fire pit?

Dry, seasoned hardwood such as oak, ash or birch. Never burn treated timber, painted wood or rubbish.

Can I put a fire pit on a wooden deck?

Only with a proper fireproof base designed for the purpose. Otherwise place the pit on gravel, stone or pavers away from the deck.

How long do fire pit ashes stay hot?

Embers can stay hot for many hours. Let ashes cool completely, preferably overnight, before disposing of them.
