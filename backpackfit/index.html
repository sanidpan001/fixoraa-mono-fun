Why Torso Length — Not Height — Decides Pack Size

Walk into any outdoor shop and the first thing a good fitter does is ignore your height. Two people can both be 5'10" and need completely different packs, because what matters is torso length: the distance from the bony bump at the base of your neck (the C7 vertebra) to the top of your hip bones (the iliac crest). A long-legged person with a short torso wears a small pack; a short person with a long torso wears a large. Height is a rough proxy at best — torso length is the actual measurement pack frames are built around.

This is the single most common fitting mistake in backpacking. A pack sized by height rides wrong: the hip belt lands on the soft waist instead of the hips, the shoulder straps carry all the weight, and by mile six your shoulders are screaming. Get the torso size right and the pack almost fits itself — the rest is fine-tuning straps.

How to Measure Your Torso

You need a flexible tape measure and ideally a friend. Stand straight, feet together, head level.

  1. Find C7: tilt your head forward and feel for the prominent bony bump at the base of your neck — the vertebra that sticks out furthest. That is your top landmark.
  2. Find the iliac crest: place your hands on your hips, thumbs forward; the top edge of the hip bone you feel under your thumbs is the bottom landmark. It sits roughly level with your belly button on most people.
  3. Measure: run the tape down your spine from C7 to the iliac crest line, following the curve of your back. Stand naturally — do not arch or slouch.
  4. Record in inches (pack sizing is an imperial holdout even in metric countries). Repeat twice and average.

Typical adult torsos run 15–23 inches. This tool maps your number to the industry-standard frame sizes: XS (under 16"), S (16–18"), M (18–20.5"), L (20.5–22"), and XL (22" and up). If you fall exactly on a boundary, size up if you carry heavy loads (more frame to distribute weight) and size down if you prioritize agility.

The 80/20 Rule: Hips Carry, Shoulders Steer

A properly fitted pack puts 70–80% of the weight on your hips via the hip belt and only 20–30% on your shoulders. The hip belt must sit on the iliac crest — the bony shelf of the pelvis — not above it on the soft waist and not below it on the thighs. When you tighten it, you should feel the weight settle onto your hips and your shoulders lighten immediately. If tightening the hip belt does nothing, the pack is either the wrong torso size or the wrong belt size.

Strap order matters when you put the pack on: hip belt first (snug, centered on the iliac crest), then shoulder straps (snug but not crushing — you should be able to slide a flat hand under them), then load lifters (the small straps from the top of the shoulder straps to the frame, angled at roughly 45 degrees), and finally the sternum strap (to keep shoulder straps from sliding outward, positioned about an inch below the collarbones). Reverse the order when taking weight off at a rest stop.

Hip belts come in sizes too, and they are swappable on most quality packs — a medium frame with a large hip belt is a completely normal combination. Belt size follows your waist/hip circumference, not your torso size, so do not assume they match.

Load Range: Matching Pack Volume to Trip Length

Frame size (torso) and pack volume (liters) are independent choices. A medium torso can carry a 35L weekend pack or an 85L expedition hauler — the frame fits your body, the volume fits your trip. As a rough guide:

  • Daypacks (15–35L): day hikes, hut-to-hut with resupply, climbing approaches. Torso fit still matters, but the margin for error is wide with light loads.
  • Weekend packs (35–55L): 1–3 nights with a tent, bag, and food. The sweet spot for most backpackers.
  • Multi-day packs (55–75L): 3–7 nights, colder weather, bulkier gear.
  • Expedition packs (75L+): winter, mountaineering, or carrying group gear. Demands precise torso fit — small errors become big pain under 25kg.

A classic beginner error is buying too much volume 'just in case.' Empty space invites overpacking, and a half-full 75L pack carries worse than a full 50L. Buy for the trips you actually take, not the expedition you might take someday.

Daypack vs Expedition: The Picker Logic

The daypack-vs-expedition decision comes down to three variables: duration (nights out), self-sufficiency (tent and food vs huts and restaurants), and season (winter gear is dramatically bulkier). A summer overnighter with hut accommodation needs barely more than a large daypack; a self-supported winter week needs expedition volume. The tool's picker walks through these three questions and lands on a volume class — then reminds you that the torso size underneath it does not change. The same medium frame that fits you in a 30L daypack fits you in a 70L hauler from the same brand.

Fit Troubleshooter: Symptoms and Fixes

Shoulder pain within the first hour

Almost always a torso-size or hip-belt problem. If the frame is too long, the hip belt sits too low and the shoulders take everything; if too short, the belt rides high and the shoulder straps saw at your neck. Re-measure your torso and check belt position on the iliac crest before blaming the pack.

Lower-back ache

Often the load lifters: they should pull at about 45 degrees. If they are flat (horizontal), the frame is too short for you; if they point steeply down, it is too long. Also check that heavy items are packed close to your back and between shoulder-blade and hip height.

Hip bruising

The belt is either too loose (slipping and sawing) or positioned on soft tissue instead of bone. Tighten decisively — hip belts are meant to be cinched hard — and confirm the padding, not the buckle, is over the iliac crest.

Numb fingers or tingling arms

Shoulder straps too tight or the sternum strap too high, compressing nerves near the collarbone. Loosen the shoulder straps, lower the sternum strap, and let the hip belt take the load.

Pack sways side to side

Compression straps are loose, or the load is packed asymmetrically. Cinch the side compression straps and repack heavy items centered. On frameless ultralight packs, some sway is normal — it is the price of minimalism.

Trying Packs in the Shop: The Weighted Test

Never judge a pack empty. Shops keep sandbags or weighted pillows for exactly this reason — load the pack with 8–12kg (roughly a real weekend load), put it on in the strap order above, and walk the shop floor for ten minutes, including stairs if available. Pay attention to: pressure points on the shoulders, whether the hip belt stays put when you lean forward, and whether the load lifters sit at 45 degrees. Ten honest minutes beats ten online reviews. And wear the boots and layers you will actually hike in — a fitting done in a t-shirt lies about winter clearances.

Women's, Youth, and Non-Standard Fits

Women's-specific packs are not marketing: they typically run shorter torso ranges, narrower shoulder straps set closer together, a shorter hip belt with more conical shaping, and a sternum strap positioned to avoid compression. Youth packs scale all of this down with adjustable torso panels to allow for growth. If you are between standard sizes, broad-shouldered, or carry weight unusually, look for packs with adjustable torso suspension — a sliding harness that covers 3–5 inches of range. Adjustable does not mean universal, but it forgives a borderline measurement.

Care, Lifespan, and When to Replace

A quality pack lasts 500–1,000 trail days. Rinse off salt and sweat, never machine-wash (it destroys waterproof coatings), and store uncompressed with buckles fastened. Replace when the hip-belt foam collapses permanently (the most common death — the belt looks fine but no longer grips), the frame stays bent, or the suspension has stretched beyond adjustment. And re-measure your torso every few years: bodies change, and the pack that fit at 25 may not fit at 40.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure my torso for a backpack?

Tilt your head forward and feel for the prominent bump at the base of your neck (the C7 vertebra). Measure from there down your spine to the top of your hip bones (iliac crest), following your back's curve. That number in inches is your torso length — most adults measure 15–23 inches.

Does torso size really matter more than height?

Yes — it is the single most important fitting measurement. Packs are built around torso length, not height: two people of the same height can need different pack sizes. A wrong torso size puts the hip belt in the wrong place and dumps the load onto your shoulders.

How much pack weight should sit on my hips vs shoulders?

About 70–80% on your hips via the hip belt, and only 20–30% on your shoulders. If your shoulders ache within an hour, the hip belt is either mispositioned, too loose, or the pack's torso size is wrong — not a sign you need stronger shoulders.

What if my torso measurement falls between two sizes?

Size up if you regularly carry heavy loads (the longer frame distributes weight better) and size down if you prioritize agility and a snug fit for scrambling or skiing. If you are truly on the line, try both weighted in a shop — ten minutes of walking decides it.

How tight should the hip belt be?

Tighten it decisively onto the iliac crest (the top of the hip bones), not the soft waist. The padding — not the buckle — should sit on the hip bones, and the pack should not shift when you lean. You should feel weight lift off your shoulders the moment it is snug.

What pack volume do I need for a weekend trip?

Daypacks: 15–35L. Weekend trips: 35–55L. Multi-day: 55–75L. Expedition/winter: 75L+. Torso size and volume are independent — the same medium frame fits you whether the pack is 30L or 70L.
