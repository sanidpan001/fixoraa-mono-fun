Carry-On Size Limits by Airline: The Complete Guide

Every year, millions of travelers ask the same anxious question at the departure gate: will my bag fit as carry on? The honest answer is that there is no single global carry-on size. Each airline publishes its own maximum dimensions, its own weight limit, and its own rules about what counts as a "personal item" — and the differences are big enough to ruin a trip. A suitcase that glides through a US domestic sizer can fail spectacularly on a European budget carrier, and a bag that is fine on a full-service airline can be overweight on a Gulf carrier that weighs every cabin bag at check-in. That is why carry on size limits by airline have become one of the most-searched travel topics in the world, and why BagFit exists: to check your bag against 44 airlines in one click, before you pack, not after you are arguing with a gate agent.

The core problem is fragmentation. The International Air Transport Association once suggested a guideline of 55 × 35 × 20 cm for cabin bags, and many airlines cluster near it — but it was only ever a suggestion, and plenty of carriers deviate. US legacy airlines such as American, Delta and United allow roughly 56 × 35 × 23 cm, while Southwest and Alaska stretch to 61 × 41 × 25 cm, among the most generous allowances anywhere. Cross the Atlantic and the picture changes: Lufthansa, SWISS, Austrian, SAS and Finnair converge on 55 × 40 × 23 cm with an 8 kg weight limit, while Air France and KLM shave the width to 55 × 35 × 25 cm — one centimeter narrower than most hard-shell rollers on the market. Budget carriers play a different game entirely. Ryanair's free allowance is only a 40 × 30 × 20 cm small bag; the larger 55 × 40 × 20 cm cabin bag requires a paid Priority upgrade. Wizz Air and easyJet follow the same two-tier model, and in Asia, AirAsia enforces a strict 7 kg combined limit for your cabin bag and personal item together. The Gulf carriers — Emirates, Qatar Airways and Etihad — all cap economy cabin bags at 7 kg and weigh them routinely, and Qatar's 50 cm maximum length is the shortest of any major carrier, which disqualifies a surprising number of "international carry-on" suitcases sold in the United States.

How Airlines Actually Measure Your Bag

Before you ask "will my bag fit as carry on," you need to know what airlines measure — because it is almost certainly more than the number printed on the retail tag. Airlines measure the bag's longest points in every dimension, including wheels, handles, feet and bulging side pockets. That sleek 55 cm suitcase with 4 cm spinner wheels is really a 59 cm suitcase, and the gate sizer does not care what the manufacturer claimed. Retractable handles that protrude, expandable zippers left open, and overstuffed front pockets all add centimeters that count against the limit. Hard-shell bags fail most often on the depth axis: a 20 cm limit is unforgiving when the handle housing sticks out the back. Soft duffels and backpacks have the opposite advantage — staff sometimes allow a soft bag that compresses into the sizer, though you should never count on discretion. When you measure, stand the bag exactly as it will be presented at the gate, extend nothing, zip everything, and measure with a rigid tape across the widest points. Then add a one-centimeter safety margin in your head, because sizers are manufactured to the published limit with zero tolerance, and a bag that is exactly 56.0 cm in a 56 cm sizer is a coin flip.

Will My Bag Fit as Carry On? The Three-Minute Method

The fastest reliable way to answer "will my bag fit as carry on" takes three minutes and a tape measure. First, measure length, width and height at the widest points, wheels and handles included, and note the bag's packed weight on a bathroom scale. Second, enter those numbers into BagFit and run the reverse lookup against all 44 airlines — the result grid tells you instantly which carriers your bag fits and which it does not, and by how many centimeters on each axis. Third, check the one axis that usually fails: depth. Across the dataset, the depth limit is the most common deal-breaker, because US bags are built around 9-inch (23 cm) depths while much of Europe and Asia caps depth at 20 cm. If your bag is a 56 × 36 × 23 cm US-standard roller, it fits American, Delta and Southwest, but it fails Lufthansa's 8 kg-adjacent world only on weight — and it flatly fails Ryanair's 20 cm depth, Korean Air's 20 cm depth, and Emirates' 20 cm depth. Fourth, remember rotation: a bag fits if it fits in any orientation, which is exactly how BagFit compares dimensions — your three numbers are sorted against the airline's three sorted limits, so you never have to guess which side goes where in the sizer. Finally, re-measure after packing. A bag that measured 19 cm empty can measure 23 cm stuffed with souvenirs, and that transformation happens on the return leg, when you are least prepared for a gate fee.

Airline Personal Item Size: The Free Bag Most Travelers Waste

Here is the travel hack hiding in plain sight: almost every airline lets you bring a second bag for free. The airline personal item size — the backpack, tote, laptop bag or large purse that must fit under the seat in front of you — is a separate allowance on top of your carry-on, and most travelers underuse it. Typical airline personal item size limits run 40 × 30 × 15 cm on European and Asian full-service carriers, 43 × 25 × 22 cm on United, 45 × 36 × 20 cm on easyJet, and a generous 46 × 43 × 20 cm on Spirit. The under-seat space is genuinely smaller than the overhead bin, which is why the limits are tighter, but a well-chosen 35–40 liter travel backpack fits almost every personal-item sizer in the world while swallowing a laptop, a change of clothes, toiletries and snacks. There are traps, though. On Ryanair, Wizz Air and Vueling basic fares, the personal item is your only free bag — the full-size cabin bag costs extra. On AirAsia and Scoot, the 7–10 kg weight limit covers your carry-on and personal item combined, so loading the backpack does not dodge the scale. And ANA, JAL and Qantas apply the same combined-weight logic. BagFit's dual check exists for exactly this: enter both bags, pick your airline, and see both verdicts side by side, because the personal item that fits under a US seat can be too tall for a European one.

Multi-Leg Trips: The Strictest Airline Always Wins

The most expensive carry-on mistake is not checking the wrong airline — it is checking only the first airline. On a multi-leg itinerary, your bag must satisfy every carrier on the ticket, and the strictest airline on your route is the one that decides whether your bag flies free. Consider a common booking: New York to Bangkok with a US legacy carrier across the Atlantic and a Gulf or Asian carrier onward. Your 56 × 36 × 23 cm bag clears the US leg comfortably, then meets Qatar Airways' 50 × 37 × 25 cm limit and 7 kg scale — and fails on length by six centimeters. Or picture London to Sydney via the Middle East: the European leg tolerates 55 × 40 × 23 cm, but the onward carrier's 20 cm depth limit ends the discussion. BagFit's multi-leg mode automates this by taking the element-wise minimum across every leg you select: the smallest allowed length, the smallest allowed width, the smallest allowed depth, and the lowest weight limit — each labeled with the airline that imposes it. If your bag clears that composite limit, it clears your whole trip. If it does not, you know exactly which leg is the problem and can plan around it: check the bag through, repack into a smaller case, or choose a different routing. One more subtlety: code-shares. The operating carrier's rules apply, not the airline whose logo is on your ticket, so always verify which metal you are actually flying.

Carry-On Weight Limits: The Rule Nobody Reads

Travelers obsess over dimensions and ignore weight, which is backwards on half the world's airlines. US and Canadian carriers generally publish no carry-on weight limit — if you can lift it into the overhead bin, it flies. But step outside North America and weight enforcement is real and routine. The 8 kg club — Lufthansa, SWISS, Austrian, SAS, Finnair, TAP — weighs cabin bags at check-in counters on a large share of flights. The 7 kg club — Emirates, Qatar, Etihad, Singapore Airlines, Cathay Pacific, Air India, IndiGo, AirAsia — is stricter still, and on AirAsia and Scoot the limit covers both bags combined. British Airways is the famous exception at 23 kg, which is effectively unlimited for any sane packing job, while Hawaiian enforces 11.3 kg (25 lb) per piece, a quirk of its inter-island operation. Where is the scale? Usually at the check-in counter, sometimes at the gate on full flights, and almost always when your bag looks heavy. The counter-move is simple: wear your heaviest items, put dense electronics and books in your personal item or coat pockets during weighing, and know your airline's number before you arrive. BagFit shows each airline's weight rule and enforcement note alongside the size verdict, because a bag that fits the sizer but fails the scale still ends up in the hold — and still costs you.

Oversize Bags, Gate Checks and Fees

When a bag fails the sizer at the gate, the airline gate-checks it: a tag is printed, the bag goes into the hold, and you pay. Gate-check fees are the most painful kind because they are set at the moment you have zero leverage, and they are almost always higher than pre-booked hold luggage. On US legacy carriers, expect roughly $30–$75 for an oversize gate check; on European low-cost carriers, €30–€60 at the gate versus half that online; and Spirit and Frontier can charge $65–$100+ when the fee is assessed at the boarding door. The pattern is consistent: the cheaper the ticket, the more aggressive the enforcement and the steeper the fee, because ancillary revenue is the business model. There is also the "borderline" zone — a bag 1–2 cm over — where outcomes depend on the agent, the fullness of the flight, and whether the sizer is even out that day. BagFit's fee estimator models this honestly: a fitting bag costs nothing, a borderline bag is flagged as agent's discretion, and clearly oversize bags get an estimated range with the caveat that low-cost carriers multiply everything. The cheapest strategy is boring but unbeatable: measure before you buy the bag, check every leg of the trip, pre-book hold luggage online if you are over, and never discover your bag is oversize at the gate.

Soft Duffels vs Hard-Shell Rollers

Your bag's construction changes the odds. Hard-shell rollers protect contents and roll beautifully, but they are dimensionally honest: what you measure is what the sizer sees, and the handle housing plus spinner wheels silently add 3–5 cm beyond the shell size. Soft duffels and travel backpacks compress, which buys forgiveness at the sizer — a 58 cm duffel cinched to 55 cm often passes where a 58 cm hard case cannot. The trade-off is that soft bags expand when overpacked, and an overstuffed duffel can fail worse than a hard case. Wheels deserve special attention: two-wheel rollers hide their wheels in the body profile and measure truer to the tag size, while four-wheel spinners add height that counts. If you fly budget carriers regularly, consider a "sizer-proof" bag: a soft-sided 55 × 40 × 20 cm case with recessed wheels. It is the largest rectangle that fits Ryanair, easyJet's paid cabin bag, Korean Air, Emirates and Air India simultaneously — the closest thing to a universal carry-on that exists. And whatever you buy, the manufacturer's "cabin size" or "carry-on approved" label is marketing, not a guarantee: it usually means "fits some airlines," and BagFit's reverse lookup will tell you exactly which ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the standard carry-on size for international flights?
There is no single global standard, but 55 × 40 × 20 cm is the closest thing to a universal safe size — it fits Ryanair, easyJet's paid cabin bag, Korean Air, Emirates and Air India. US carriers are more generous (typically 56 × 35 × 23 cm), while Qatar Airways allows only 50 cm in length. BagFit checks your exact bag against 44 airlines so you do not have to memorize any of this.
Do airlines measure wheels and handles in carry-on dimensions?
Yes — airlines measure the bag's longest points including wheels, handles, feet and protruding pockets. A suitcase tagged "55 cm" with 4 cm spinner wheels is really 59 cm, and the gate sizer will catch it. Always measure the bag as it will be presented at the gate, fully zipped, and include every protrusion.
What is the difference between a carry-on and a personal item?
A carry-on goes in the overhead bin (typically up to 55–56 cm long); a personal item must fit under the seat in front of you (typically 40 × 30 × 15 cm). Most airlines allow one of each. On budget carriers like Ryanair and Wizz Air, the personal item may be your only free bag — the full-size cabin bag costs extra.
What happens if my bag is 1 cm over the limit?
A bag 1–2 cm over is in the "agent's discretion" zone: on a quiet day it may pass, on a full flight it will be gate-checked and you will pay the oversize fee. Sizers are built to the published limit with zero tolerance. BagFit flags borderline results separately so you know you are gambling, not guessing.
Do connecting flights use the strictest airline's baggage rules?
Effectively, yes — your bag must satisfy every carrier on your itinerary, so the smallest limit on each axis governs. A bag that fits your US domestic leg can fail on the onward Gulf or Asian leg.
