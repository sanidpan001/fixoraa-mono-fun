About this tool

A cheese board looks effortless when it works, and it works because of quantities, not luck. The difference between a board that feels generous and one that looks picked-over after ten minutes is usually a matter of grams per person, decided before shopping. Cheese is rich, salty and filling, so guests eat less of it than they would of bread or salad, but they also graze for longer, which means the board needs to last. Planning by guest count is the only reliable way to get that balance right.

The standard appetiser portion is about 85 grams of cheese per person, with 60 grams of charcuterie and 45 grams of crackers alongside it. If the board is the main event, a grazing dinner with little else, raise the cheese to about 125 grams per person and add more bread, fruit and nuts to match. For light pre-dinner nibbles, 60 grams of cheese per person is plenty, because guests will eat a full meal afterwards. These figures assume a mixed board; a cheese-only board without charcuterie needs slightly more cheese, because there is nothing else to fill the gap.

Variety matters as much as weight, and the classic rule is one soft, one hard and one blue or flavoured cheese as a minimum. Soft cheeses like brie and camembert give creaminess, hard cheeses like aged cheddar and manchego give savoury depth, and a blue or a washed-rind cheese gives a talking point. For larger groups, add a goat cheese and a smoked cheese to reach five varieties, but do not go beyond five, because too many choices confuse guests and each cheese gets too little attention. Buy the varieties in roughly equal weights, except for blue, which most guests eat in smaller amounts.

Charcuterie follows its own logic. Cured meats are salty and intense, so 60 grams per person is generous, and a mix of two or three types, such as prosciutto, salami and chorizo, covers most tastes. Fold and ribbon the slices rather than laying them flat, because volume makes a board look abundant even when the weight is modest. Include at least one non-pork option if your guest list is mixed, such as bresaola or a smoked turkey, and keep vegetarian guests in mind with extra cheese, nuts and marinated vegetables rather than an empty corner of the board.

Crackers and bread are the delivery system, and they deserve real thought. Offer two cracker types, one plain and one seeded or wholegrain, plus a sliced baguette or sourdough for guests who prefer bread. Crackers go stale quickly once opened, so buy sealed packs and open them just before serving, and keep a spare pack in reserve for a long evening. About 45 grams of crackers per person is the right planning figure, which works out to roughly a small handful each, plus bread on top if the board is a main meal.

Accompaniments are what turn a pile of cheese into a board. Grapes, apple slices and dried apricots add sweetness and acidity that cut through fat, while nuts add crunch and honey or chutney adds a sweet-savoury bridge. Olives and cornichons give briny contrast, and fresh herbs like rosemary make the board look finished with almost no effort. Budget about 30 grams of accompaniments per person in total, spread across three or four items, and place them in small piles rather than one large bowl, so guests can reach everything without crossing the board.

Board logistics are simple once the quantities are settled. One board serves about eight guests comfortably; beyond that, use two boards or a large platter, because crowding makes grazing awkward and messy. Take cheese out of the fridge thirty to sixty minutes before serving, because cold cheese tastes muted and waxy, while room-temperature cheese is creamy and aromatic. Label the cheeses with small cards or a note, because guests love knowing what they are eating, and labels prevent the awkward moment when someone with an allergy has to ask about every item.

Shopping for a cheese board rewards a short list and a good cheesemonger. Tell the monger your guest count and your budget, and let them suggest one cheese you would not have chosen yourself, because a surprising cheese is often the one guests remember. Buy cheese a day or two ahead and store it wrapped in wax paper, not cling film, so it can breathe without drying out. Avoid pre-sliced supermarket cheese packs for the main selection, because they look uniform and taste dull next to a proper wedge cut to order.

Dietary needs shape the board more than most hosts expect. Pregnant guests should avoid soft mould-ripened cheeses and unpasteurised varieties, so include hard cheeses and pasteurised options clearly labelled. Lactose-intolerant guests can often eat aged hard cheeses, which are naturally low in lactose, but they will appreciate a note. Gluten-free guests need gluten-free crackers kept separate from crumbs, and vegan guests need a plant-based cheese or a generous section of nuts, fruit and dips so they are not left with crackers alone.

The best cheese board is one where the host is not refilling it every ten minutes or apologising for an empty corner. Weigh the cheese, count the guests, choose three to five varieties, and let the board do the rest. CheeseBoard turns that planning into one number, so you can spend your energy on choosing interesting cheeses and arranging them beautifully, which is the fun part. A board that looks abundant, tastes varied and lasts the evening is a quiet triumph, and your guests will remember it long after the last cracker is gone.

A last word on presentation, because a cheese board is eaten with the eyes first and the arrangement matters as much as the quantities. Place the largest items, the cheese wedges and the bowls of olives or honey, first, spacing them around the board like anchor points, and then fill the gaps with charcuterie folds, cracker fans and fruit clusters. Work from large to small, leaving no empty patches, because a full board looks abundant while a sparse board looks apologetic even when the weight is identical. Add height with a small raised dish or a folded linen napkin under one corner, and finish with a scattering of fresh herbs and a drizzle of honey over the blue cheese just before serving. Photograph the board if you like, because a beautiful board is a fleeting artwork, and then step back and let your guests discover it.

One more practical detail is worth settling before shopping: the knife and serving situation, because the wrong tools turn a beautiful board into a struggle. Provide one knife per cheese type where possible, or at least one for soft cheeses and one for hard cheeses, because mixing a blue cheese knife into a brie contaminates flavours and looks careless. Small spoons for honey, chutney and olives keep sticky fingers out of shared dishes, and a few cocktail sticks help guests serve themselves neatly. If your board includes a runny cheese like a ripe camembert, serve it in a small dish or on a leaf of baking paper, because a runny cheese that escapes across the board swallows crackers and neighbouring cheeses alike. These details cost almost nothing and signal that the board was assembled by someone who cares.

Frequently asked questions

How much cheese per person for a cheese board?

About 85 grams per person for an appetiser board, 125 grams if the board is the main meal, and 60 grams for light nibbles.

How many cheese varieties should I serve?

Three as a minimum, one soft, one hard and one blue or flavoured, and up to five for larger groups.

How much charcuterie per person?

About 60 grams per person, across two or three types such as prosciutto, salami and chorizo.

How many guests does one cheese board serve?

One board serves about eight guests comfortably; use two boards or a large platter for bigger groups.

Should cheese be served cold or at room temperature?

Take cheese out of the fridge 30 to 60 minutes before serving, because room-temperature cheese tastes far more aromatic.

What accompaniments go on a cheese board?

Grapes, apple slices, dried fruit, nuts, honey or chutney, olives and cornichons, about 30 grams per person in total.
