    Why Pruning Time Matters More Than Pruning Technique

    Most pruning damage is not caused by bad cuts — it is caused by good cuts at the wrong time. Prune a lilac in February and you have neatly removed every flower bud it spent last summer building. Prune an oak in May in wilt country and you have opened a doorway for a fatal disease. Prune a pine back into bare wood and that branch stays bare forever, because pines cannot resprout from old stems the way a rose can.

    The calendar above encodes the single most important pruning rule: match the cut to the plant's growth cycle. Plants that bloom on old wood — lilacs, forsythia, azaleas, spring clematis — set their flower buds the previous year, so they must be pruned right after they finish blooming. Plants that bloom on new wood — roses, panicle hydrangeas, butterfly bush, crepe myrtles — flower on the current season's growth, so they get pruned in late winter before growth starts. Get that one distinction right and you will never again wonder why a shrub that looks healthy refuses to bloom.

    The Two Questions Behind Every Pruning Decision

    Before you pick up the shears, answer two questions. First: does this plant bloom on old wood or new wood? Old-wood bloomers (most spring-flowering shrubs) are pruned after flowering, typically late spring to early summer. New-wood bloomers (most summer-flowering shrubs and roses) are pruned in late winter while dormant. Second: is this plant currently dormant, actively growing, or setting buds? Dormancy — late winter for most deciduous plants — is the safest window for structural work because the plant is not spending energy, wounds are exposed to fewer pathogens, and you can actually see the branch structure without leaves in the way.

    Evergreens play by slightly different rules. Needled evergreens like pines and junipers are pruned as new growth extends in spring — you pinch candles rather than sawing branches. Broadleaf evergreens like boxwood and holly are sheared in early summer after the spring flush hardens off. The common thread: prune evergreens when they are actively growing so they can seal wounds quickly, not in fall when new growth would face winter unprepared.

    Late Winter: The Main Event

    For most gardeners, late winter — roughly February into March in the northern hemisphere — is pruning season. Fruit trees (apple, pear), grapes, roses, butterfly bush, crepe myrtles, and panicle hydrangeas all get their structural pruning now. The logic is consistent: the plants are dormant, disease pressure is low, and every cut directs the coming spring's energy exactly where you want it.

    This is also when you do the hardest cuts of the year. Rejuvenation pruning — cutting an overgrown shrub back hard to restart it — belongs in late winter because the plant has a full growing season to recover. Butterfly bush can go to twelve inches. Overgrown roses can lose a third of their canes. The plant will look alarming for about three weeks and then explode. The one limit: never remove more than about a quarter to a third of a woody plant's total mass in one year, with the exception of plants like butterfly bush that are built for hard cutting.

    After Blooming: The Spring-Flowering Crew

    Lilacs, forsythia, azaleas, camellias, and spring clematis share a deal with the gardener: they bloom early on wood grown last year, and in exchange you prune them immediately after the flowers fade. This gives them the maximum possible time to grow the wood that will carry next year's show. Wait until fall and you are cutting off buds; wait until winter and you are cutting off the entire spring display.

    The technique for this group is thinning, not shearing. Remove the oldest stems at ground level — about a third of them each year on a three-year rotation — and the shrub perpetually renews itself with young, floriferous wood. Shearing the outside into a ball, by contrast, creates a shell of foliage over a dead interior and steadily reduces flowering. The ugliest old lilac in the neighborhood is almost always a sheared one.

    Summer: Light Touch Only

    Summer pruning is for maintenance, not renovation. Pinch spent flowers to encourage rebloom (roses, butterfly bush), shorten the whippy new growth on wisteria to about six leaves, and remove water sprouts and suckers while they are small and easy. Summer is also when you prune spring-bleeding trees like maples and dogwoods — with leaves on and sap flow settled, cuts bleed little and seal fast.

    What you do not do in summer is heavy structural work. Big wounds made in hot weather are slow to compartmentalize and invite disease, and removing large amounts of foliage starves the roots at the peak of their energy-gathering season. One more summer rule: stop shearing hedges like boxwood by late summer. Every shear triggers a flush of tender growth, and growth made in September gets killed by the first hard frost.

    Fall: Mostly, Put the Shears Down

    Fall is the season for the least pruning. Cuts made in fall stimulate regrowth that winter then destroys, and open wounds enter the wettest, most disease-friendly months of the year. The exceptions are small and specific: remove genuinely dead, damaged, or diseased wood any time you see it (the three Ds are always in season), and cut back the mushy foliage of spent perennials after frost.

    Fall is, however, the season for planning. Walk the garden with a notebook while the structure is still visible and write down what each plant needs in late winter. Order replacement blades. Disinfect and oil your tools. The gardeners with the best-pruned plants in March are the ones who planned in November.

    The Never-Cut List: Warnings Worth Memorizing

    Every plant has a characteristic mistake, and the calendar flags each one. A few deserve emphasis because they are irreversible. Cutting a pine or juniper back into brown, needle-less wood leaves a permanent dead stub — conifers mostly cannot resprout from old wood. Cutting lavender into old woody stems does the same. Topping a crepe myrtle (the infamous crepe murder) does not kill it but creates ugly knuckles and weak regrowth every year after. Pruning oaks during wilt season can kill a century-old tree to save an afternoon.

    The pattern: the worst mistakes come from treating all plants alike. Roses forgive; conifers do not. Spring bloomers punish winter pruning; summer bloomers require it. When in doubt about a plant you do not know, the safe move is always the same: remove only the dead, damaged, and diseased wood, and look up the rest before you cut.

    Tools and Cuts That Heal

    Good timing deserves good tools. Bypass pruners (the scissor type) for live stems — anvil pruners crush. Loppers for branches up to two inches, a sharp pruning saw beyond that. Disinfect between plants, and always between cuts on a diseased plant: a quick wipe with rubbing alcohol or a ten-percent bleach solution prevents you from being the disease vector. Sharpness matters more than brand — a clean cut compartmentalizes in weeks; a crushed, ragged cut invites rot for seasons.

    Cut to a bud, a branch, or the branch collar — never leave a stub. Stubs die back and become entry points for decay; the branch collar (the slight swelling where branch meets trunk) contains the chemicals that seal the wound, so cutting just outside it is the fastest-healing cut you can make. On roses and cane shrubs, cut a quarter inch above an outward-facing bud so new growth heads away from the center.

    Adjusting for the Southern Hemisphere

    Every month in the calendar shifts by six for gardeners south of the equator: February becomes August, dormant-season work moves to July and August, and after-bloom pruning follows the local flowering season rather than the calendar. The tool's hemisphere toggle does this automatically. The underlying logic never changes — dormant plants in their winter, spring bloomers after their bloom — only the month names move.

    TaskNorthern hemisphereSouthern hemisphere
    Dormant structural pruningJan – MarJul – Sep
    Spring bloomers (after bloom)Apr – JunOct – Dec
    Summer maintenanceJun – AugDec – Feb
    Hands offOct – DecApr – Jun

    Within each hemisphere, adjust for your climate: gardeners in mild-winter regions prune a few weeks earlier than those with harsh winters, and high-altitude gardeners later. The calendar gives you the window; your last frost date fine-tunes it.

    Frequently asked questions

    When should I prune roses?

    In late winter (February to March in the northern hemisphere), while dormant. Remove dead and crossing canes, cut the rest back by about one-third above an outward-facing bud. Never cut below the graft union at the base.

    Why didn't my lilac bloom this year?

    The most common cause is pruning at the wrong time. Lilacs bloom on old wood and set next year's buds in summer, so pruning in fall or winter removes the flowers. Prune lilacs right after they finish blooming in late spring.

    When is the best time to prune apple trees?

    In late winter dormancy (January to February), when you can see the branch structure and disease pressure is lowest. Remove crossing branches and water sprouts, and never remove more than 25% of the canopy in one year.

    Can I prune in the fall?

    Mostly no. Fall cuts stimulate tender regrowth that winter kills, and wounds enter the wettest disease season. The exceptions: always remove dead, damaged, or diseased wood, and cut back frost-killed perennial foliage.

    What does 'blooms on old wood' mean?

    It means the plant forms its flower buds on last year's growth. Spring bloomers like lilac, forsythia, and azalea are old-wood bloomers — prune them right after flowering. Summer bloomers like roses flower on new growth and are pruned in late winter.

    How do I adjust pruning times for the southern hemisphere?

    Shift every month by six: northern February becomes southern August. Use the hemisphere toggle in the tool. Dormant pruning happens in your winter (July–August), and spring bloomers are pruned after their local bloom season.
