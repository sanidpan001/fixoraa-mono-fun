How to Win at Connect Four: Strategy for the Classic Drop Game

Connect four is a strategy game that hides surprising depth behind a child's toy. Two players drop coloured discs into a vertical grid, and the first to line up four in a row wins. The rules take ten seconds to learn, but the game has been solved by mathematicians, studied by game theorists and played competitively for decades — all because a simple grid and a simple goal create a huge number of possible positions.

The most important idea in connect four is that the board is not flat. Discs fall to the lowest empty space in a column, so you cannot play any square you like; you can only play the top of each column's stack. That gravity rule changes everything. A threat in the air is not a real threat until the squares below it are filled, and a clever player thinks in terms of which squares will become playable next, not just which squares look good right now.

The centre column is the most valuable place on the board. A disc in the centre can be part of horizontal lines in both directions, vertical lines below it and diagonals in several directions. Opening in the centre gives you the most future options, which is why strong players almost always start there. The computer in this game prefers the centre too, so expect a fight for the middle columns in the early moves.

Threats are the currency of connect four. A threat is three discs in a row with an empty square that would complete four. When you create a threat, your opponent must block it on the next move or lose. Creating two threats at once — a double threat — is usually decisive, because the opponent can only block one. Much of advanced play is about setting up double threats quietly, so that by the time they appear, it is too late to stop both.

Blocking is equally important, and the most common beginner mistake is to ignore the opponent's threats while chasing your own. Every move should be checked against one question: if I play here, does my opponent win next turn? If the answer is yes, you must block, no matter how attractive your own plan looks. The computer in ConnectFour follows this rule — it wins when it can and blocks when it must — so games against it teach good defensive habits quickly.

Vertical threats deserve special attention because they are easy to miss. Four discs stacked in a column look less dramatic than a horizontal line, but they win just as surely. Watch for columns where your opponent already has two or three discs stacked, and remember that playing on top of their stack may be the only way to block them. Similarly, do not casually drop a disc into a column that completes your opponent's vertical line.

Diagonal lines are the hardest to spot and therefore the most satisfying to build. A diagonal threat often hides among other discs, and players focused on rows and columns can miss it entirely. When scanning the board, make a habit of checking diagonals after every move — your own and your opponent's. The few seconds it takes can save a game that looked comfortably won.

Another key concept is the odd and even threat. Because discs fall, some squares can only be played on odd-numbered turns and others only on even-numbered turns, depending on how many discs are already in the column. Advanced players use this to predict who will get to a key square first. You do not need to master the theory to enjoy the game, but noticing that a threat square will arrive on your opponent's turn rather than yours is a powerful insight.

For children, connect four is an excellent first strategy game. It teaches planning, patience and the idea that an opponent has plans too. Losing a game because you missed a diagonal is a gentle lesson in attention, and winning by setting a trap teaches the joy of thinking ahead. The game is short, the feedback is immediate, and the next match starts in seconds.

Whether you play casually or competitively, the best advice is the same: control the centre, create threats, block theirs, and always check the diagonals. Play a few games against the computer, watch how it blocks your threats, and copy the habits that beat you. Before long, you will be setting double threats of your own — and winning games you once would have lost.

Connect four proves that a game does not need complexity to be deep. Seven columns, six rows and one goal are enough to create a lifetime of interesting positions. Drop your first disc, aim for the centre, and enjoy the quiet battle of wits that follows.

Connect four was solved mathematically in the late 1980s, when researchers proved that the first player can force a win with perfect play. That result does not make the game boring; it makes it fascinating, because it means the starting position contains a forced win that no human can fully calculate. Casual games remain wide open, because neither player plays perfectly, and the gap between theory and practice is where all the fun lives.

One famous concept is the seven trap, where a player creates a threat that can only be blocked by playing in a column that then enables another threat. Traps like this are the connect four equivalent of a chess combination: a sequence of moves that looks innocent until the trap closes. Learning to spot traps, and to avoid walking into them, is the step from casual to serious play, and it comes from studying your losses rather than just your wins.

The game also has a rich tournament history, with world championships and computer programs that play at superhuman level. Watching strong players compete is instructive, because they think several moves ahead and talk about threats, parity and tempo in ways that casual players rarely consider. Even a few minutes of watching expert play can transform how you see the board, especially the importance of the bottom rows.

For families, connect four has a practical advantage over many strategy games: games are short, setup is instant, and a younger player can genuinely win against an adult by spotting a threat the adult missed. That keeps the game fair across ages in a way that chess, for example, rarely is. A child who wins a game of connect four has earned it, and that earned win builds confidence for harder games later.

Finally, the physical version of the game teaches something the digital version can hide: the sound and feel of discs dropping, and the visual pleasure of a board filling up. The digital version compensates with instant resets and a tireless opponent, so you can play dozens of games in the time one physical game would take. Both versions reward the same skills, and both are worth playing.

A good practice habit is to replay your lost games from memory, or by noting the final board, and to find the move where the game turned. Usually there is a single missed block or a missed double threat that decided the outcome. Finding that move is more valuable than playing ten new games, because it corrects a specific weakness rather than just adding experience.

Another habit is to play with a simple rule for a few games: always check diagonals before every move, no exceptions. The rule feels slow at first, but after a dozen games it becomes automatic, and your results improve noticeably. Deliberate rules like this are how casual players become strong players, one habit at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you win connect four?

Connect four discs of your colour in a row — horizontally, vertically or diagonally — before your opponent does.

Why is the centre column important?

A centre disc can form lines in more directions than an edge disc, giving you more winning chances and more threats later in the game.

What is a double threat?

Two threats at once. Your opponent can only block one on their next move, so the other usually wins the game for you.

Does the computer play well?

The computer wins when it can, blocks your winning threats, and prefers the centre. It is a solid practice opponent for casual and intermediate players.

Can children play ConnectFour?

Yes. The rules are simple, games are short, and it is a great introduction to strategy and planning.

Is ConnectFour free?

Yes, completely free with unlimited games and no signup.
